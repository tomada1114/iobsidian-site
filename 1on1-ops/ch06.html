Is a testing strategy just 'write more unit tests'?

After this page you can explain what each kind of test buys, and at what price. You can also say why fakes usually beat mocks, and how to test a feature whose output isn't fixed.

About 8 min readPairing at Leo's desk

In this page

  • What does each kind of test buy?
  • Pyramid or trophy?
  • Mocks, fakes or recordings?
  • How do you test output that isn't fixed?

Leo's desk. His pull request added thirty unit tests, and the reviewer still asked how he knows it works.

01What each test buys

L

Leo: I mocked every collaborator of the command handler. Coverage is 100% on that file. And the reviewer asks, "How do you know it works?"

M

Maya: What do your tests check?

L

Leo: That the handler calls the repository's save method once, with the right card.

M

Maya: And if the repository's save is broken?

L

Leo: Then… my tests still pass. The mock always says yes.

M

Maya: Right. Coverage tells you which lines ran. It doesn't tell you what was checked.

L

Leo: So 100% can mean very little.

M

Maya: It can. Let's step back. Each kind of test buys a different kind of confidence, at a different price.

L

Leo: What are the kinds, for us?

M

Maya: Unit tests check pure rules. Our domain package is plain functions, so they run in milliseconds and give the same answer every time.

L

Leo: Like scheduling the next review of a card.

M

Maya: Right. Then contract tests: does an adapter keep its promise against a real engine?

L

Leo: And component tests render a screen?

M

Maya: Under a fake browser, with a stand-in for the API. Then one smoke test, which asks whether the built system answers over HTTP.

L

Leo: No browser tests that click through the app?

M

Maya: None, on purpose. That's a decision, not something we forgot.

L

Leo: Why not? They'd prove the most.

M

Maya: They're slow, and they fail for reasons that have nothing to do with your change. Speed and determinism are part of the price.

L

Leo: Because a slow test stops being run.

M

Maya: And a flaky one stops being believed. Either way, it stops protecting you.

02Pyramid or trophy

L

Leo: I learned the test pyramid. Many unit tests, fewer integration tests, very few end-to-end.

M

Maya: That's Mike Cohn's shape. Kent C. Dodds drew another one, the testing trophy.

L

Leo: What's different?

M

Maya: Static checks form the base: types and lint. And the most weight sits on integration tests, not unit tests.

L

Leo: So which one is right?

M

Maya: Neither is a law. The shape follows the architecture.

L

Leo: How does it follow ours?

M

Maya: Our domain is pure, so unit tests there are cheap and meaningful. In a codebase where every rule touches the database, they wouldn't be.

L

Leo: And static checks?

M

Maya: Types and lint catch a whole class of bugs before any test runs. A wrong field name never gets that far.

L

Leo: And the seams between layers?

M

Maya: That's where contract tests and the smoke test go. The seams are where the pieces disagree.

L

Leo: How are the tests organised?

M

Maya: Into five projects, by what they touch. Unit and component tests get five seconds each. They do no I/O, so a hang is a bug.

L

Leo: And the others?

M

Maya: Automation tests spawn processes, so they get two minutes. The smoke suite needs a build, and the DynamoDB suite needs a container, so each runs on its own.

L

Leo: So a fast run on every save, and slower runs where they're needed.

M

Maya: That's the idea.

L

Leo: What would a hang look like in a unit test?

M

Maya: Say a rule waits on a promise that never resolves. With no I/O, nothing should wait, so five seconds already means something is wrong.

03Mocks, fakes and recordings

L

Leo: So what should my handler tests look like instead?

M

Maya: What's the difference between a mock and a fake, in your words?

L

Leo: Isn't a fake just a mock with more code?

M

Maya: No. A mock checks that calls happened. A fake is a real, simpler implementation, like an in-memory store.

L

Leo: So with a fake, the handler really saves, and I can read the card back.

M

Maya: Right. Your code runs the same path it runs in production.

L

Leo: What goes wrong with the mock, apart from what we saw?

M

Maya: It breaks when you refactor, even though behavior is unchanged. Rename the method, and thirty tests fail.

L

Leo: While the app still works.

M

Maya: And the reverse: it can pass while the real thing is broken. That's the dangerous half.

L

Leo: And a recording?

M

Maya: It replays a real response, captured once. It's useful for external APIs and for model calls.

L

Leo: So what's the rule here?

M

Maya: Mock only at boundaries: the network, the filesystem, the clock and randomness. Prefer fakes. Assert behavior, not call counts.

L

Leo: What's the catch with fakes?

M

Maya: You must prove the fake behaves like the real thing. Otherwise it's a second system that can lie.

L

Leo: How do you prove that?

M

Maya: Contract tests. The same cases run against the fake and against the real engine, and both must pass.

L

Leo: So if the fake drifts, a case fails.

M

Maya: Right. The fake earns its trust by passing the same cases.

L

Leo: So our API tests drive real Request objects over the in-memory store.

M

Maya: Right. And the web screens render under a fake browser with a stand-in fetch. Nothing checks how many times a method was called.

L

Leo: What about the coverage number, then?

M

Maya: We keep floors per area, so a gap gets noticed. But a floor only warns you about gaps. It doesn't prove anything works.

04When the output isn't fixed

L

Leo: What about LLM grading? The same answer might get two different explanations.

M

Maya: So a test can't assert one exact output. What can it check?

L

Leo: That the response has the right shape?

M

Maya: Right. Tests still check the plumbing: the schema, the parsing, and that a retry doesn't grade twice.

L

Leo: Against a fake or a recording, I guess.

M

Maya: Both. The same code can run with a fake, a recording or the real model.

L

Leo: And the quality of the grades?

M

Maya: That's measured statistically, by evaluations. How often do the verdicts agree with gold labels, marked by a person?

L

Leo: And how often does the verdict flip, if you ask twice?

M

Maya: That's the other number. The evaluation gates CI against a baseline, so a worse prompt can't merge quietly.

L

Leo: So tests for plumbing, evals for quality.

M

Maya: That's the split Quickturn uses for grading. It works for any feature where the answer isn't fixed.

05Leo explains it back

M

Maya: Write your reply to the reviewer. What do you say?

L

Leo: Each kind of test buys a different confidence at a different price, so we pick a mix, not a shape.

L

Leo: Our pure domain makes unit tests cheap, and the seams get contract and smoke tests.

L

Leo: I'll replace the mocks with the in-memory store, and mock only at boundaries.

M

Maya: The grader is the awkward case.

L

Leo: For AI features, tests check the plumbing and evals measure quality.

M

Maya: Add the coverage point. A 100% line tells the reviewer what ran, and nothing about what was checked.

Summary in three lines

  1. Each kind of test buys different confidence at a different price; choose the mix by where the risk is.
  2. Prefer fakes to mocks, mock only at boundaries, and assert behavior rather than calls.
  3. When output isn't fixed, tests check the plumbing and evaluations measure quality.
