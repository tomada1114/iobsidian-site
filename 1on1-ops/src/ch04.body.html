How reliable is reliable enough?

After this page you can explain what an SLI, an SLO and an SLA are. You can also show how an error budget turns reliability into something you spend. And you can say how to choose a target without guessing.

About 8 min readAfter the design review

In this page

  • What are an SLI, an SLO and an SLA?
  • Why not aim for 100%?
  • What does an SLO cost, and what does it buy?
  • What does reliable mean for an AI agent?

After Tuesday's design review. Someone from product asked how reliable the app is, and nobody had a number.

01Three terms from the ground up

L

Leo: That question from product was awkward. I think we should just promise 99.99% uptime. Higher is better.

M

Maya: Promise it to whom?

L

Leo: To learners, I suppose. Isn't that what an SLO is? A promise to customers?

M

Maya: Not quite. Let's build the words from the bottom. Start with what you can measure.

L

Leo: Requests that worked, and requests that didn't.

M

Maya: Right. An SLI, a service level indicator, is a ratio: good events divided by all events.

L

Leo: What counts as good?

M

Maya: You decide, and write it down. For example, API requests answered without a 5xx, in under a set number of milliseconds.

L

Leo: So the SLI is just a measurement.

M

Maya: Yes. The SLO, the objective, is your target for that measurement over a window. Say, 99.5% over 28 days.

L

Leo: Is that our number?

M

Maya: No, those numbers are only an example for today. The point is the shape: a ratio, a target, a window.

L

Leo: And the SLA?

M

Maya: A service level agreement is a contract with a penalty. If you miss it, you owe someone money or credits.

L

Leo: So my 99.99% was an SLO.

M

Maya: Right. It's an internal goal, and nobody owes money for missing it.

L

Leo: Why would the SLA be different from the SLO at all?

M

Maya: It's usually looser. You want to notice a problem, and fix it, before you owe anyone anything.

02The error budget

L

Leo: Fine, but why set the target lower than 99.99%? Wouldn't a higher target make us try harder?

M

Maya: Turn it around. What is 100% minus the SLO?

L

Leo: With 99.5%, that's 0.5%. The failures we're allowed.

M

Maya: That's the error budget. It's how much failure you may spend in the window.

L

Leo: Spend on what? Failure isn't something you want.

M

Maya: On change. Every release, every migration, every risky experiment carries some chance of failure.

L

Leo: So if there's budget left, we can take risks.

M

Maya: Right. Ship faster, try the bigger change. If the budget is gone, you slow down and fix reliability first.

L

Leo: And who decides that?

M

Maya: A rule we agree on in advance. Then the number decides, and nobody has to argue about whether this week feels unstable.

L

Leo: But still, why not aim for 100%?

M

Maya: Two reasons. First, users can't tell 99.99% from 100%. Their own phone network fails more often than that.

L

Leo: So a learner on a train sees failures we never caused.

M

Maya: Right. Second, each extra nine costs far more work than the last one. More regions, more failover, slower releases.

L

Leo: And the learner never notices the difference.

M

Maya: That's the trade. You'd pay a lot for reliability nobody can see.

L

Leo: Then how do you pick the number?

M

Maya: Start from what users notice. For Quickturn, a card must appear, and an answer must save.

L

Leo: What about speed? The drill has a timer.

M

Maya: The timer runs on the client, so a slow API doesn't shorten a learner's time to answer. A failed save hurts far more than a slow one.

L

Leo: So the save matters most.

M

Maya: Then you measure first, before you set anything. You set the target near what you already achieve, and tighten it later.

L

Leo: Why not start ambitious?

M

Maya: A target you already miss leaves no budget at all. Then it stops steering anything.

03What an SLO costs

L

Leo: Writing "99.5%" in a document takes a minute. Where's the cost?

M

Maya: You're partly right. An SLO is cheap on paper. The expensive part is measuring it honestly and acting on it.

L

Leo: Measuring honestly how?

M

Maya: Agreeing what "good" means is harder than it sounds. Is a 4xx a failure? Is a request that took eight seconds a success?

L

Leo: A 4xx is usually the client's mistake.

M

Maya: Usually. But if our own bug sends a 400 to every learner, the SLI must catch it.

L

Leo: And acting on it?

M

Maya: When the budget is gone, you slow releases. That's a real cost, and someone has to accept it.

L

Leo: Where does Quickturn measure its SLOs?

M

Maya: In prod only, from the API's request outcomes. On dev, the only traffic is the team's own, so the ratio means nothing.

L

Leo: Ten requests from me testing a broken branch.

M

Maya: Right. That would burn the budget in an afternoon, for no learner.

L

Leo: And in prod, what happens when it burns?

M

Maya: An alert fires when the error budget burns fast. It watches a failure rate over a window, not single events.

L

Leo: So do we still need the symptom alarms and the dashboard?

M

Maya: Yes. They catch sudden breakage. The SLO catches slow erosion, like a failure rate that creeps up over two weeks.

L

Leo: So what does the SLO buy us?

M

Maya: An agreed number for one hard question: is this bad enough to stop shipping?

L

Leo: Is the SLO the only reliability check in prod?

M

Maya: No. Restore drills run there too, so we know a backup really comes back.

04Reliability for an AI agent

L

Leo: Does this work for an AI agent? It rarely answers with a 5xx.

M

Maya: That's the trap. An agent can be available all day and still fail its users.

L

Leo: How?

M

Maya: It answers, but it doesn't finish the task, and a person has to step in.

L

Leo: So what would the SLI be?

M

Maya: The share of tasks completed without a human rescue. And the share of responses under a set number of seconds, end to end.

L

Leo: Why stress end to end?

M

Maya: A task might take eight model calls. Each call can be fast, and the task can still take a minute.

L

Leo: So a per-call latency SLO would look green while users wait.

M

Maya: Right. Measure what the user waits for, not what's easy to count.

L

Leo: Same rule as the alarms, then.

M

Maya: It is. Start from what the user notices, and work back to a number.

05Leo explains it back

M

Maya: Product will ask again next Tuesday. What will you say?

L

Leo: An SLI is a good-events ratio. The SLO is our target for it, and an SLA is a contract with money attached.

L

Leo: The gap between the SLO and 100% is an error budget we can spend on change.

L

Leo: We measure our SLIs in prod, set the SLO near what we already achieve, and tighten from there.

M

Maya: Why not 99.99%?

L

Leo: Learners can't see the difference, and each nine costs far more.

M

Maya: Also tell them what "good" means in our SLI, in one sentence. That definition is where most arguments start.

Summary in three lines

  1. An SLI is a measured ratio, an SLO is the target for it, and an SLA is a contract.
  2. 100% minus the SLO is an error budget, spent on change and protected when it runs out.
  3. Measure first, set the target near today's level, and tighten; for agents, measure whole tasks.
