What should wake someone up at night?

After this page you can explain why alarms should watch what users feel, not every cause you can measure. You can also say how to prove an alarm works, and when one alarm per event becomes noise.

About 8 min readThe morning after an alarm email

In this page

  • Should alarms watch symptoms or causes?
  • How do we know an alarm really fires?
  • When does a sensitive alarm become noise?
  • What belongs on the one dashboard?

Morning. Leo got his first alarm email overnight, and it turned out to be nothing.

01Symptoms, not causes

L

Leo: I got an alarm email at 2 a.m. The Lambda function was throttled. I checked, and everything was fine by then.

M

Maya: How did that feel?

L

Leo: Awful, then silly. So should we add more alarms, to be safe? Or remove this one?

M

Maya: What does "safe" mean to you here?

L

Leo: That we catch every problem. CPU, memory, every metric we have.

M

Maya: Who feels high CPU?

L

Leo: Nobody, directly. A learner feels a slow page, or an error.

M

Maya: That's the key split. A symptom is what users feel. A cause is one of many reasons behind it.

L

Leo: And causes are what we can measure most easily.

M

Maya: Right, which is why teams alarm on them. But a cause can spike while every learner is fine.

L

Leo: Like my throttle last night.

M

Maya: Possibly. We'll come back to that one.

L

Leo: Why not alarm on causes as well? More warning can't hurt.

M

Maya: It can. Each cause alarm is another email that may mean nothing. And a symptom alarm already catches any cause that turns into errors for users.

L

Leo: Is there a list of symptoms worth watching?

M

Maya: The Google SRE book names four golden signals: latency, traffic, errors and saturation. Saturation means how full something is.

L

Leo: Four sounds manageable.

M

Maya: Tom Wilkie's RED method fits a request-driven service like our API: rate, errors and duration. Brendan Gregg's USE method fits resources, like a table or a queue: utilization, saturation and errors.

L

Leo: So RED for the API, USE for DynamoDB.

M

Maya: That's how we use them. Now map our five alarms onto them.

L

Leo: The API's 5xx alarm is errors, and it's the symptom a learner sees. Function errors are errors too.

M

Maya: And the other three?

L

Leo: Function throttles, and table read and write throttles. Those are saturation.

M

Maya: Right. Let me draw where each one sits.

02An alarm you never saw fire

L

Leo: How do we know these alarms work at all? Last night was the first email I ever got.

M

Maya: Not from one email. An alarm you have never seen fire is only a hope.

L

Leo: So we break something on purpose?

M

Maya: On dev, yes. We set the API function's reserved concurrency to 0, so it could run no copies at all.

L

Leo: Then every request fails.

M

Maya: We sent five requests, and all five answered 503. Within about two minutes, the 5xx alarm and the throttle alarm went into ALARM.

L

Leo: And the emails arrived?

M

Maya: Both of them. Then we removed the setting, and the API came back.

L

Leo: Why does the email matter so much? The alarm already changed state.

M

Maya: Because an alarm nobody receives is silent. The topic's email address is passed in at deploy time.

L

Leo: And if a deploy runs without it?

M

Maya: The subscription is removed. The alarms still change state, but no email goes anywhere.

L

Leo: That sounds easy to miss.

M

Maya: It was. So the deploy workflow prints a warning when the address is missing, rather than failing quietly.

L

Leo: So the proof has two halves: the alarm fires, and the message reaches a person.

M

Maya: And you repeat the test after anything that touches the path.

L

Leo: Like a change to the topic, or to the deploy workflow.

M

Maya: Right. It takes five minutes, and it tells you the one thing you can't learn any other way.

03Sensitivity and fatigue

L

Leo: Back to my 2 a.m. email. Why did one throttle wake me?

M

Maya: Look at the settings. Each alarm fires on a single event in one minute: threshold 1. Missing data counts as OK.

L

Leo: So one throttled call is enough.

M

Maya: On dev, with one user, that's right. Any 5xx there is worth a look.

L

Leo: But with thousands of learners?

M

Maya: One 5xx in a minute is noise. You'd get emails every night, and you'd learn to ignore them.

L

Leo: And then a real one gets ignored too.

M

Maya: That's alarm fatigue. It's how real incidents get missed.

L

Leo: So what changes at scale?

M

Maya: You alarm on rates or ratios over longer windows. Say, more than two percent of requests failing over ten minutes.

L

Leo: And in prod?

M

Maya: In prod, Quickturn alerts when the error budget burns fast against its reliability targets. Dev keeps the threshold-one alarms.

L

Leo: Different rules for different places.

M

Maya: Because the question is different. On dev, "did anything break?" In prod, "are learners hurting?"

L

Leo: Is there a quick test for whether something deserves an alarm at all?

M

Maya: Two questions. Does a user feel it? And is there a clear first action?

L

Leo: And if the answer is no?

M

Maya: Then it shouldn't page anyone. It's a dashboard line, a runbook to write, or it gets deleted. Every alarm needs an owner and a first thing to do.

L

Leo: Where does my throttle land?

M

Maya: On dev, it's a useful early sign. In prod, one throttle is a dashboard line. The alarm there watches a sustained rate, because saturation turns into errors next.

04One dashboard

L

Leo: So what goes on the dashboard?

M

Maya: API requests, API errors with 5xx and 4xx, and API latency at p90. Then function errors and throttles, function duration at p90, and table throttles.

L

Leo: Why p90 and not the average?

M

Maya: An average hides the slow tail. If one learner in five waits four seconds, the average can still look fine.

L

Leo: And p90 shows that one in five.

M

Maya: Right. Ninety percent of requests were faster than that number.

L

Leo: Does all of this cost money?

M

Maya: The whole set is sized to fit CloudWatch's free tier: ten alarm metrics and three dashboards, as of 23 September 2026.

L

Leo: And if someone adds a sixth alarm without thinking?

M

Maya: A test pins the set at five alarms and one dashboard. Adding one means changing the test, so a reviewer asks why.

L

Leo: Does this apply outside a web API? Say, an internal AI agent.

M

Maya: The idea does. There, a 5xx is rarely what the user feels.

L

Leo: What do they feel, then?

M

Maya: "The task didn't finish," or "it took far too long." So you alarm on the task failure rate and on step timeouts.

L

Leo: Same rule, different symptom.

M

Maya: Right. Start from what the user would complain about, then find the metric.

05Leo explains it back

M

Maya: So, add alarms or remove one? Answer your own question.

L

Leo: Alarm on what users feel: errors, latency and saturation. RED for the API, USE for resources like the table.

L

Leo: Force every alarm to fire once, so you know the path to your inbox works.

L

Leo: Threshold-one alarms are fine for one user on dev. At scale they cause fatigue, so switch to rates.

M

Maya: And the rest?

L

Leo: If nobody would act on it, it shouldn't page anyone.

M

Maya: One more piece: check the email subscription after every deploy. A missing address turns every alarm silent.

Summary in three lines

  1. Alarm on symptoms users feel, using RED for services and USE for resources.
  2. Force each alarm to fire once, and confirm the message reaches a person.
  3. Single-event alarms suit dev; at scale use rates, and page only when there's a clear first action.
