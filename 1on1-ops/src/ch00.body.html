Engineering Readers: Operations and Quality

A learner reports a lost answer after a release. Quickturn needs evidence to investigate it, checks that protect changes and a recovery path for its data.

12 pagesAbout 44 minEnglish, B2 · explanatory prose

The example application

Quickturn is a fictional language-learning application. A learner answers a card before a timer ends, then reviews progress and mistakes. Its web, iOS and Android clients share an API.

These chapters use that application to explain concrete engineering choices, their costs and the conditions that would justify a different design. Each chapter stands on its own; no conversation or earlier chapter is needed.

How the pages fit together

The pages

  1. Isn't observability just a fancy word for monitoring? — Monitoring answers known questions; observability lets you ask new ones.
  2. What makes a log line actually useful? — One structured line per request, with fixed fields and no payloads.
  3. What should wake someone up at night? — Alarm on what users feel, and prove each alarm fires.
  4. How reliable is reliable enough? — An SLO turns reliability into a budget you can spend on change.
  5. How do we see what a model call costs? — Record every call with its model, prompt version, tokens and cost.
  6. Is a testing strategy just 'write more unit tests'? — Each kind of test buys different confidence at a different price.
  7. How do we know the fake database behaves like the real one? — One contract suite runs against every store.
  8. How do you test things that aren't plain code? — Generated files, templates, types and imports each get a cheap check.
  9. What actually stops a bad change from merging? — Layered gates running the same scripts, and nobody weakens them.
  10. Should every merge go straight to production? — Continuous delivery isn't auto-deploy: dev deploys itself, prod waits for a person.
  11. What happens to a learner's data when things go wrong? — Each guard covers a different failure, chosen per stage.
  12. What has to be true before we open to the public? — A readiness list, plus what you chose to skip.

How to read this

Read a chapter in a few minutes, using the diagrams and code examples alongside the explanation. The summaries collect the main decisions; the examples show where those decisions apply.
