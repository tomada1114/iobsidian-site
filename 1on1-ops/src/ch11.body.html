What happens to a learner's data when things go wrong?

After this page you can name the guards against different kinds of data loss, and say which failure each one covers. You can also explain why deleting and exporting an account are features, not scripts.

About 9 min readThe whiteboard room

In this page

  • Doesn't AWS back up the table for us?
  • Which guards does each stage get?
  • What did the team accept, and what does recovery cost?
  • How do deletion and export work?

The whiteboard room, the week a friend of Leo's told him about a deleted production table.

01Different failures, different guards

L

Leo: A friend's company deleted a production table by accident last week. But AWS backs up DynamoDB automatically, right? So we're safe.

M

Maya: What do you think it backs up, exactly?

L

Leo: Everything, I assumed. Isn't that the point of a managed database?

M

Maya: A plain table has none of the guards turned on. Each one is a decision. And each covers a different failure.

L

Leo: What failures are there?

M

Maya: Start with a bug that writes wrong data. Every learner's review dates shifted by a day, say.

L

Leo: The table is still there. It's just wrong.

M

Maya: For that, you want point-in-time recovery. You restore the table to any second within a recovery window.

L

Leo: Why not just fix the dates with another script?

M

Maya: If you know exactly what the bug did, you might. Usually you don't, and a restore to the minute before the release is the only honest answer.

L

Leo: How long is the window?

M

Maya: From 1 to 35 days, as of 23 September 2026. And the restore goes into a new table, not over the old one.

L

Leo: And my friend's case, someone deleting the table?

M

Maya: That's deletion protection. The delete call is simply refused until someone turns the protection off.

L

Leo: Couldn't a stack change delete it too?

M

Maya: That's the third failure. A stack change can replace the table, or a stack deletion can remove it.

L

Leo: And the guard for that?

M

Maya: A CloudFormation Retain policy. The table stays even when the stack says otherwise.

L

Leo: And a learner who wants to leave?

M

Maya: That's not a failure, but it's the fourth case. Their rights need a guard of their own: delete my account, export my data.

A bug writes wrong data
Point-in-time recovery, into a new table.
Someone deletes the table
Deletion protection refuses the call.
A stack would replace or remove it
A Retain policy keeps the table.
A learner asks to leave
Deletion and export, as features.
L

Leo: So each failure has one main guard.

M

Maya: And they don't stand in for each other. Deletion protection won't undo a bad write.

L

Leo: And recovery won't stop someone deleting the table.

M

Maya: It would help you afterwards, into a new table. But the app would be down until you pointed it there.

02Choices per stage

L

Leo: Which ones do we have?

M

Maya: Retain on delete and on replacement, in every stage. Deletion protection and point-in-time recovery are on in prod from the table's creation, and off in dev.

L

Leo: Why from creation? Couldn't you turn them on later?

M

Maya: You could, but the window only starts when recovery is on. A gap there is a gap you can't restore.

L

Leo: And the user pool?

M

Maya: It has deletion protection in every stage, dev included.

L

Leo: Why dev too? It's only our data.

M

Maya: A new pool issues new user ids. Every learner's data is keyed by the old ones, so it would all be stranded.

L

Leo: Even ours.

M

Maya: Even ours. Rebuilding the table from nothing is easy. Matching old data to new ids isn't.

L

Leo: So the pool is almost more precious than the table.

M

Maya: In one sense, yes. The table can be restored from a backup. A pool's user ids can't be issued again.

L

Leo: How do we know the settings stay that way?

M

Maya: A test asserts each setting for both stages. Turning one off fails the build.

03The accepted risk

L

Leo: So in dev, a bad write can't be undone?

M

Maya: No. And the table can be deleted from the console.

L

Leo: That sounds like a mistake someone should fix.

M

Maya: It's a decision someone wrote down. Dev holds only the owner's own practice history.

L

Leo: And recovery costs money.

M

Maya: It's priced by table size. For one person's history, it wasn't worth it.

L

Leo: So "accepted risk" means somebody chose it, on purpose, in writing.

M

Maya: Right. An oversight is a risk nobody knew about. This one has a name and a reason.

L

Leo: Is recovery a cost in any other way?

M

Maya: Privacy. In prod, the recovery window bounds how long a deleted learner's data can still be restored.

L

Leo: Because a restore could bring it back.

M

Maya: So the privacy policy has to state that period. A learner deletes their account, and a copy survives in the window.

L

Leo: I'd never have thought of that as a cost.

M

Maya: Most people don't. Every copy you keep is a copy you have to explain.

L

Leo: And we trust the restore works?

M

Maya: A backup you never restored is a hope. So we run restore drills: restore prod into a new table, and check what comes back.

L

Leo: Check it how?

M

Maya: Count the items, read a few learners' histories, and time the whole thing. The time matters when learners are waiting.

04Deletion and export as features

L

Leo: How does deleting an account actually work?

M

Maya: Every learner's data lives in one partition of one table.

L

Leo: So deleting it is one query?

M

Maya: One query over that partition, plus the identity mapping item that links their login to their id. Export works the same way.

L

Leo: Who runs it? An engineer, with a script?

M

Maya: No. They're product features, with tests. The learner asks, and the app does it.

L

Leo: Why does that matter, if the script works?

M

Maya: A script runs when someone remembers it. A feature runs every time, and the privacy policy can describe it honestly.

L

Leo: What about AI products? They keep more copies.

M

Maya: That's the trap. Logs, analytics exports, embeddings, conversation memory. Each one is a derived copy.

L

Leo: And if any one is missed?

M

Maya: Then "delete my data" is a promise the system can't keep.

L

Leo: Is that why the learner model stays in DynamoDB, not in agent memory?

M

Maya: Partly. In the table, it's deleted and exported with the rest of the partition.

L

Leo: And the conversation memory itself?

M

Maya: It has its own retention, and it holds conversations, not the learner's record. That keeps the list of copies short enough to check.

05Leo explains it back

M

Maya: Your friend asks how Quickturn would survive their mistake. Tell them.

L

Leo: Each guard covers a different failure: point-in-time recovery for bad writes, deletion protection for a deleted table, Retain policies for stack changes.

L

Leo: We keep Retain everywhere, but skip recovery in dev because it's only the owner's data. That's a risk we wrote down.

L

Leo: Prod gets all of them.

M

Maya: And a learner who leaves?

L

Leo: Deleting or exporting an account is one partition, and it's a feature, not a script.

M

Maya: Also mention the drills. Until a restore has actually run, nobody knows how long a recovery takes.

Summary in three lines

  1. Recovery, deletion protection and Retain policies each guard against a different failure.
  2. Each guard is a per-stage choice; a skipped one is a risk written down, not an oversight.
  3. Deletion and export are tested features over one partition, and must reach every derived copy.
