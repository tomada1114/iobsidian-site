A good idea is not enough

Have you ever had an idea for a tool and thought, "Everyone needs this"? Many developers have felt that way. They spend weeks building something, and then they find out that nobody wants to change how they work.

The problem is not that the idea was bad. The problem is that the developer never checked one simple thing: what do people already do about this today?

What a workaround tells you

A workaround is a way of getting around a problem when there is no proper tool for it. It might be a shared spreadsheet, a long chain of emails or a text file that someone copies and pastes from every week. These ways of working are often slow and messy, but people put up with them because they have no better choice.

A workaround tells you two useful things. First, it shows that the problem is real. People who spend time on a messy fix every week care about the problem, even if they never complain about it. Second, it shows what a first version of your tool must do, because the steps in the workaround are the steps your tool needs to replace.

A shared spreadsheet as a clue

Imagine a developer who wants to build a small side product for teams that plan work shifts. Before writing any code, she asks a friend's team how they handle it. The team has a shared spreadsheet which three people edit, and every Friday one person copies the names into a chat message. Mistakes happen, and people often ask, "Which version is the right one?"

This is the difference between a "nice idea" and a problem that people already spend time on. If the team had no spreadsheet and no Friday routine, the idea might still be nice, but it would be harder to know that anyone needs it. Because the workaround exists, she knows where to start: one shared list, one clear version and an easy way to send it to the team.

One question is worth asking

You do not need a big survey to learn this. It is worth asking just one person, "How do you handle this today?" Then listen, and write down the steps that they describe, even the boring ones.

Those steps are a map of the problem. One honest description of a workaround can tell you more than a long list of ideas, and it can show you where your first version should begin.

Words and phrases to notice

workaround
a way to get around a problem without a proper tool
put up with
accept something annoying because you cannot change it
worth asking
useful enough to ask; "This book is worth reading."
side product
something you build outside your main job
routine
a thing you do the same way regularly

English to notice

Relative clauses: “The team has a shared spreadsheet which three people edit.” The words after “which” tell us more about the spreadsheet. “People who spend time on a messy fix” uses “who” for people.

Present perfect: “Have you ever had an idea for a tool?” We use “have you ever” to ask about any time in your life up to now.

Think about it

Have you ever put up with a messy workaround at work? Describe it, and say what a better tool would need to do.
