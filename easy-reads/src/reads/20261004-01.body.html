The request and the real problem

Imagine you build tools for other people at work. A teammate asks for a new button that makes a report look nicer. The request is clear, so it feels natural to open your editor and start. But what people say they want and what actually slows them down are often two different things.

People are not lying when they ask for a feature. They describe the problem in the way they can see it. While they know their own work very well, they rarely notice small steps that they repeat every day. Those steps have become normal to them, so they do not mention them.

What watching shows you

So before you build, ask to watch one person do the task the slow way. Do not explain or help. Just notice what they do and how they do it. You will often see things that never came up in the conversation.

Say you watch your teammate make the report. First, they export data from one system as a file. Then they open it, copy a few columns and paste them into a spreadsheet. They fix the dates by hand, because the two systems write dates in different ways. Then they do the same thing again for the next team. It turns out that the nicer button would save one minute, while the copying and fixing take thirty minutes every week.

Changing what you build

Now your decision is different. Instead of the button, you could build a small script that pulls the data and fixes the dates automatically, without a person doing it. The design was never the main thing that got in the way of their work; the repeated step was.

Although this kind of watching is simple, it is not the same as asking questions. When you ask people how they work, they give you a short, tidy answer. When you watch them, you see the real version, with the extra clicks, the waiting and the small fixes. This does not mean you should ignore what people ask for. Their request is still a useful clue, but it is the start of the search, not the end.

A ten-minute start

You do not need a big survey to find a good problem. Ask one person if you can watch them do a routine task for ten minutes. Write down each step, and mark every step that is repeated. Those marks often point to the problem worth solving.

Ten minutes is a small cost, and it can save you from building the wrong thing. You may also find that the person feels heard, because you took time to see their work as it really is.

Words and phrases to notice

get in the way
stop something from happening easily; e.g. Noise gets in the way of my work.
it turns out that
you learn something you did not expect; e.g. It turns out that he was right.
export
save data from one program so another can use it
routine task
a job you do the same way again and again
automatically
by itself, without a person doing it

English to notice

Indirect questions: “Just notice what they do and how they do it.” Inside a sentence, a question word is followed by normal word order: “what they do,” not “what do they do.”

While / although: “The nicer button would save one minute, while the copying and fixing take thirty minutes.” “Although this kind of watching is simple, it is not the same as asking questions.” Both words join two ideas that contrast.

Think about it

Whose routine task could you watch for ten minutes? What do you think gets in the way of their work? Try using “I want to see how they ...” in your answer.
