Three things in the cart

Imagine a developer called Ken who wants to learn a new programming language. In one evening, he finds a paid online course, a nice new keyboard and a thick book about the language. He is tempted to buy all three, because each one makes him feel that he is already moving forward.

Many of us are tempted to do the same thing when we start something new. Buying feels like action. But a purchase only helps if you actually use it, so it is worth asking one question first: what will this help me do or finish?

Think about cost per use

A useful idea here is cost per use, the price divided by the number of times you really use something. It does not tell you whether a thing is good or bad. It tells you whether it has a clear job in your plan.

Let's use simple, made-up numbers as an example. Say the course costs 100 dollars, the keyboard costs 150 and the book costs 40. If Ken watches one lesson a day for a month, he will use the course about 30 times, so each use costs a little over three dollars. If he opens the book only twice, each use will cost 20 dollars.

The keyboard is harder to judge. He will type on it every day, but his old keyboard works fine. If it does not change how much he practices, it will not help his goal at all, even though he uses it often.

A purchase needs a clear job

So the real question is not whether something is cheap or expensive. It is whether it supports the goal or only feels like progress. For Ken, the course has a clear job: it gives him a lesson to finish each day. The book might pay off later, when he needs to look up details, so he can wait and buy it then.

This way of thinking works for many choices, from tools at work to things for a hobby. You do not need to stop spending on learning. Spending on growth can pay off well, as long as you know what each purchase is for.

Your small step

Before your next learning purchase, write down how many times you expect to use it in the next month. Be honest, and choose a number you really believe. If the number is small, ask yourself what you would stop doing or finish because of it.

You can spend on growth with confidence when each purchase has a clear job. A short note before you pay can make that job easy to see.

Words and phrases to notice

be tempted to
want to do something, often without thinking (He is tempted to buy all three.)
pay off
bring a good result later (Practice will pay off.)
cost per use
the price divided by how many times you use it
purchase
something you buy
look up
search for information in a book or online

English to notice

If ..., it will ...: “If he opens the book only twice, each use will cost 20 dollars.” This first conditional talks about a real possible situation and its likely result.

Whether ... or ...: “It is whether it supports the goal or only feels like progress.” This pattern shows two possible answers to one question.

Think about it

Think of something you want to buy for learning. How often will you use it next month? Try answering with “If I use it ..., it will ...” or “pay off”.
