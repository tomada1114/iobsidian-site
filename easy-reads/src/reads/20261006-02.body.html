Why "Any feedback?" fails

Have you ever shared your work, asked "Any feedback?" and then heard nothing for two days? Or maybe you got a quick "Looks good to me!" that did not help at all. This happens a lot, and it is usually not because people do not care.

The problem is the question. "Any feedback?" asks the other person to look at everything. They have to decide where to start, what kind of comment you want and how much time it will take. Since that feels like a big job, many people put it off or give a short, polite answer instead.

Ask about one part

Here is a simple example. A developer opens a pull request, a request to add their code changes to the team's project. In the first version, she writes "Any thoughts?" In the second version, she writes, "Is the error handling in this function easy to follow? I'm on the fence about splitting it into two smaller functions."

The second question is more specific, so it is easier to answer than the first one. The reviewer knows exactly which part to look at, and they can see that it will only take a few minutes. They can also get to the point: "Yes, split it, because the second part is hard to read." That is an answer she can act on today.

Name the part and your doubt

A good question has two pieces. First, it names a part: one function, one slide, one paragraph or one number in a plan. Second, it says what you are unsure about. If you are on the fence between two choices, say both of them, because the other person can then compare them quickly.

This works outside of code, too. Instead of "What do you think of my slides?", you could ask, "Is slide three clear enough for people who are new to the project?" You can still ask for general comments later, but a narrow question gives the conversation a clear start.

Try it next time

Next time you share work, add one question that names a part and what you are unsure about. It may feel a little strange at first, but asking a clear question is a skill, and it gets easier with practice.

It also makes feedback feel less scary for both people. You know what kind of comment is coming, and the reviewer knows their help is wanted in one small, safe area.

Words and phrases to notice

pull request
a request to add your code changes to a shared project
on the fence
not able to decide between two choices yet
get to the point
say the most important thing directly
put it off
decide to do something later
act on
do something because of advice or information

English to notice

More specific / easier than: “The second question is more specific, so it is easier to answer than the first one.” Long words use “more”; short words add “-er”. Use “than” before the thing you compare with.

Because / since: “Since that feels like a big job, many people put it off.” Both words give a reason. “Since” often comes at the start, when the reason is easy to see.

Think about it

What is one part of your work you are on the fence about? Write a question you could ask a coworker, and try using “because” or “more specific than” in your answer.
