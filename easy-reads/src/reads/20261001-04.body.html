What do we see?

Q: Why do stars twinkle? On a clear night, stars seem to shake. Their light gets bright, then dim, then bright again. Some even flash with small bits of color.

But a few bright points do not do this. They shine with a calm, steady light. Most of the time, these calm points are planets. So why are stars and planets different?

Is the star really changing?

Q: Does the star change its light? No. The star shines in a steady way. The change happens near us, in the air above our heads.

Our air is always moving. Some parts are warm and some parts are cold. Warm air and cold air bend light in slightly different ways. This bending is called refraction, the way light changes direction when it goes into a new kind of material.

Starlight passes through many moving layers of air on its way to your eye. Each layer bends it a little. The layers move all the time, so the bending changes all the time. The light seems to jump around. This is called scintillation, a long word for twinkling.

Why are planets calm?

Q: The air is the same for planets. So why do they not twinkle? The answer is size. Stars are very far away. Even with a big telescope, most stars look like a single tiny point. All of the light comes from one point, so one moving bit of air can push all of it.

Planets are much closer to us. In a telescope, a planet looks like a small disk, a round flat shape. You can think of the disk as many tiny points side by side. The air pushes each point in a different way. Some get brighter while others get dimmer. Together, the changes cancel out. The light evens out, and the planet looks steady.

When does a planet twinkle?

Q: Do planets ever twinkle? Yes, sometimes. When a planet is low in the sky, its light goes through much more air. Then even a planet can flicker a little.

Stars also twinkle more near the horizon, the line where the sky meets the land. This is one reason why people build big telescopes on high mountains. There is less air above them, so the stars shake less.
