Why we feel behind

Many engineers share what they learn online. You open your feed and see someone who has built a new app in a weekend, or someone who has just started a new job. It is easy to feel that you fall behind, which means you move more slowly than other people and cannot keep up.

This feeling is common, and it does not mean you are doing badly. It often comes from the kind of comparison you are making, not from your real progress.

Two very different starting points

When you compare yourself with someone else, you mix many things together. That person may have started years before you, while you started last spring. They may want a different job, have more free time, or only post their best days.

So the comparison is not fair to either of you. Although it looks like a simple race, you are really on different roads with different goals. You also cannot change their past or their plans, so the comparison gives you worry but little to work with.

A fairer comparison

There is one person who has the same starting point and the same goals as you: yourself, three months ago. When you look back on your own work from that time, you measure the thing you can actually change.

Day by day, growth is hard to feel. You learn a little on Monday and forget a little on Tuesday, so it seems like nothing moves. But open an old note or some old code, and the change becomes clear. You may see a long function that you would now split into small parts, or a question that now feels easy to answer. Since then, you have learned more than you noticed, and you have become faster at some tasks without trying.

This does not mean you should never look at other people. Their posts can give you ideas, while your own past work tells you how you are doing.

Try one small look back

Today, open one note or file that you made about three months ago. Read it slowly, and then write one sentence about something you can do now that you could not do then. It can be small, like a command you now remember or an error you now understand.

Progress is often easier to see when you look back on your own path. The next time you feel behind, that one sentence can remind you that you are still moving forward.

Words and phrases to notice

fall behind
move more slowly than others; e.g. "I fell behind in class."
look back on
think about something in the past; e.g. "I look back on that year."
feed
the list of new posts you see on a website
comparison
looking at two things to see how they differ
measure
find out how big or how much something is

English to notice

Present perfect: “Since then, you have learned more than you noticed, and you have become faster at some tasks.” We use “have + past participle” for changes from the past up to now.

While / although: “Although it looks like a simple race, you are really on different roads.” Both words join two ideas that contrast with each other.

Think about it

Look back on something you made three months ago. What have you learned since then? Try using “have learned” or “although” in your answer.
