A night of costumes

Halloween is on October 31. In many American neighborhoods, children go out after dark that night. They wear costumes, special clothes that make them look like someone else. Some are ghosts. Some are cats, heroes or small monsters.

The children walk from house to house with a bag or a bucket. This is called trick-or-treating. Young children often go with a parent. Older children go in small groups with friends.

At the door

A child walks up to a house and knocks or rings the bell. When the door opens, the child says, "Trick or treat!" The person at the door gives the child a piece of candy. Then the child says, "Thank you," and walks to the next house.

The words sound like a threat. Long ago, they meant "Give me a treat, or I will play a trick on you." Today, nobody really plays a trick. The words are just part of the game.

The porch light

But how do children know which houses have candy? Not every family takes part. The answer is the porch light, the small light by the front door.

If the porch light is on, it means "Come and knock. We have candy." If the porch light is off and the house is dark, it means "No candy here." Children skip the dark houses and walk on. Nobody is upset about it.

Many homes also put pumpkins with faces on the front steps. These are called jack-o'-lanterns. A pumpkin with a light inside is another friendly sign. It says, "We are ready for you."

When the candy runs out

Some families buy a lot of candy, but it can still run out. When that happens, they simply turn off the porch light. The children see the dark door and go to the next house. It is a quiet and polite way to say, "We are done for tonight."

Some people leave a bowl of candy outside with a small sign. The sign may say, "Please take one." Most children take only one or two pieces.
