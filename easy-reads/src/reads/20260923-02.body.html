What does thinking aloud mean?

In many coding interviews, you solve a problem while another engineer watches. You write code on a shared screen or a whiteboard. Thinking aloud means you say your thoughts while you work. You do not sit in silence and then show the answer at the end.

For example, you might say, "First, I will look at each number once. I can keep the numbers I have seen in a set." These are simple words. They tell the interviewer where you are going.

Why does the interviewer care?

The interviewer does not only grade the final code. They also grade how you think. Can you understand the problem? Can you find a plan? Can you see the weak points of your plan? This is the real work of an engineer.

If you stay quiet, the interviewer sees only the code. They cannot tell a lucky guess from careful work. When you talk, they can see your steps. Good steps can get a good score, even if the code has a small bug.

Talking also helps you. The interviewer can give you a hint, a small push in the right direction. But they can only help if they know where you are stuck.

What should I say?

Start by saying the problem back in your own words. Ask about the input. Can the list be empty? Can the numbers be negative? These questions show that you think about edge cases, the unusual inputs at the ends of the range.

Next, say a simple plan first, even a slow one. Then say its cost, like O(n^2), and ask if you should make it faster. This is called the trade-off, the good and bad sides of a choice. After that, say each step as you write it.

What if my English is not perfect?

Short and clear is better than long and perfect. Many interviewers work with people from many countries. They listen for ideas, not for perfect grammar. Simple phrases work well: "Let me check this case." "I think this part is slow." "Can I try a different idea?"

It feels strange at first to talk and code at the same time. It gets easier with practice. You can practice alone by solving a small problem and saying every step out loud.
