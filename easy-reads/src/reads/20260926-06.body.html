Letters you do not say

Look at the word knight. It has six letters. But you only say three sounds. The k is quiet, and so is the gh.

The word though is the same. It ends in gh, but you do not hear a g or an h. Many learners find this hard. Native speakers find it hard too.

There is a reason for this. Long ago, people said these letters. The spelling stayed the same, but the way people talk changed.

How people once spoke

About a thousand years ago, English sounded very different. People said the k in knight. They said it like "k-nicht".

The gh was a real sound, too. It was a soft sound at the back of the mouth. German still has a sound like it, in words like Nacht, which means night.

So the spelling was not strange then. People wrote each word the way they said it. The letters matched the sounds.

Printing fixed the spelling

About five hundred years ago, the printing press came to England. It is a machine that puts words on paper fast. Before it, people wrote books by hand, and spelling was not fixed. One writer could spell the same word in different ways.

Printers made many copies of the same book. Over time, one spelling for each word became normal. People saw the same spelling again and again, so it stopped changing.

Speech kept moving

But the way people spoke did not stop. Over many years, the k in knight went silent. The gh sound slowly went away. Many vowels, the sounds like a, e and o, also changed.

The spelling did not follow these changes. So today, English spelling is a bit like an old snapshot, a picture of one moment in time. It shows how people spoke long ago, not how they speak now.

Software people know this problem. It is like old comments in code. The code changed, but nobody updated the comments.
