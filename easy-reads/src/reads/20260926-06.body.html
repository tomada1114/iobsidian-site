Letters we do not hear

Look at the word knight. It has six letters, but you hear only three sounds: n, the long i sound, and t. The k and gh are silent today.

The word though also ends in gh, but those letters do not make a sound now. These spellings can feel strange when you are learning English, especially if you expect every letter to be heard.

Speech changes over time

In older English, people said the k at the start of knight. The letters gh also stood for a rough sound made near the back of the mouth. Over time, both sounds changed or disappeared from these words.

The letters can stay in place as people keep using familiar spellings. Not every silent letter has the same story, but some are clues to how people once spoke.

Printing helped spread spellings

Before printing became common in England, writers could spell the same word in different ways. Printers used a printing press to make many copies of a page. This helped the same written forms reach more readers.

Although printing helped spelling become more regular, people kept changing how they spoke. By the late 1500s, many spellings had become more stable, while English sounds continued to shift. Shared spellings also help readers recognize a word across pages and places. The press influenced spelling, but it did not freeze every word in one moment.

Use spelling as a clue

An old spelling can preserve part of a word's history, even when its sound has moved on. That history may explain a pattern, but it cannot tell you the pronunciation of every English word. Some spellings have other histories, too, so one clue does not explain every unusual word.

When a word surprises you, check its sound in a dictionary or listen to an example. You can remember the spelling and the sound together. With practice, words like knight become easier to recognize without trying to say every letter.
