A small word everywhere

You say it many times a day. You say it at work, on the phone and in chat. People in many countries use it, even when they speak other languages. The word is OK.

But where did it come from? For a long time, nobody knew for sure. Many people had ideas. Some said it came from another language. Then a language expert looked at old newspapers and found a better answer.

A joke in Boston

In the 1830s, young writers in Boston liked a strange game. They made short forms of words, and they spelled the words wrong on purpose. It was a kind of joke spelling. Readers found it funny.

For example, they wrote "OW" for "oll wright," a funny way to write "all right." They wrote "KY" for "know yuse," which meant "no use." Most of these forms lived for a short time. Then people forgot them.

In 1839, a Boston newspaper printed "OK" in this way. It stood for "oll korrect," a joke spelling of "all correct." So the very first OK meant just what it means today: everything is fine.

An election helps

Most joke words died. OK did not. One big reason was an election, a time when people vote for a leader, in 1840.

That year, President Martin Van Buren wanted to win again. He came from a town called Kinderhook, in New York. His friends called him "Old Kinderhook." His team used "OK" as a slogan, a short phrase that is easy to remember. There were OK clubs, and people shouted OK at meetings.

Van Buren lost the election. But the word won. Now many people knew it, and they kept using it.

From paper to the world

Over the next years, OK moved into daily life. Workers wrote it on papers to show that something was checked. Later, it went into business, travel and movies.

Today OK is in many languages. Software uses it too. Think of the OK button in a dialog, or a 200 OK answer from a web server. A word that started as a joke now tells machines that all is well.
