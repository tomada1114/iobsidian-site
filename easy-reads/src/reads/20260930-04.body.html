What happens up high?

Q: Does water boil at the same heat everywhere? No, it does not. Near the sea, water boils at 100 degrees Celsius. High in the mountains, it boils at a lower heat. On a very tall mountain, it can boil at about 70 degrees.

This surprises many people. The water looks the same. It makes the same big bubbles. But it is not as hot.

The air pushes down

Q: Why does the water boil sooner? The answer is air. Air has weight, and it pushes down on everything. We call this push air pressure, the weight of the air above us.

Near the sea, there is a lot of air above you. So the air pressure is high. It pushes hard on the top of the water. Up on a mountain, there is less air above you. The push is weaker.

To boil, water must make bubbles of steam, or water gas. The bubbles have to push back against the air. When the air pushes less, the bubbles form more easily. So the water boils at a lower heat.

Why dinner takes longer

Q: If the water boils sooner, why is cooking slower? This is the part people often miss. Boiling water cannot get hotter than its boiling point. Extra heat from the fire only makes more steam. It does not make the water hotter.

So on a mountain, your "boiling" water is maybe 90 degrees, not 100. Food cooks by heat. Less heat means slower cooking. Pasta stays hard in the middle for longer. Rice needs more time, too.

People who live high up know this well. They cook pasta for a few more minutes. They often add a little more water, because more water turns to steam and goes away.

A pot with a lid

Q: Is there a way to fix this? Yes. Many kitchens in high places use a pressure cooker. This is a strong pot with a tight lid. The steam stays inside, so the push on the water gets stronger.

With more push, the water must get hotter before it boils. It can go above 100 degrees. So food cooks fast again, even on a mountain. The same idea works at home near the sea, too. That is why a pressure cooker can make beans soft so quickly.

Words to notice

air pressure
the push from the weight of the air above us
steam
water that has turned into a gas
boiling point
the heat at which a liquid starts to boil

a strong pot with a tight lid that holds in steam
