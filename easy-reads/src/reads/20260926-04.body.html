A full feeling in the air

A plane begins to descend, or move down, and your ears feel full. Sounds may seem quiet, as if you are underwater. Then you swallow and hear a small pop. The feeling often eases. The fullness comes from pressure, not water inside the ear.

You may notice the same pressure on the way down a mountain road. A change in height changes the air pressure around you, and your ears need time to adjust.

Pressure on both sides

Behind the eardrum is a small space called the middle ear. It contains air. The eardrum separates that space from the air outside your ear, and it moves when sound reaches it.

A narrow passage called the Eustachian tube connects the middle ear to the back of the nose and upper throat. It is usually closed. When outside pressure changes quickly, pressure inside the middle ear may take longer to change. The difference can bend the eardrum a little and make sounds seem muffled. A blocked tube can make it harder for the pressures to become equal.

A small tube opens

When you swallow or yawn, the Eustachian tube opens for a moment. Air can move into or out of the middle ear. This helps make the pressure on both sides of the eardrum more equal.

As the pressure becomes more balanced, the eardrum can move more freely. You may hear a pop as the pressure changes. The sound does not mean that anything has broken.

Help your ears adjust

Swallowing, yawning, or chewing gum may help the tube open. Sipping water can also make you swallow. When the plane is preparing to land, try to stay awake so you can notice the pressure and swallow if needed.

A cold or blocked nose can make the tube harder to open, so ear discomfort may be stronger. If pain is severe or does not ease after a few hours, contact a health professional.
