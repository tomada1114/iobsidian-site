Small numbers that hide

Imagine a developer who pays for a few small tools each month: a note app, a cloud service, a design tool and a music plan. Each one is charged to her card automatically, so she rarely thinks about them. Fifteen dollars here and ten dollars there do not feel like much.

The problem is not that these tools are bad. Many of them are useful. The problem is that a monthly price is shown in a small number, and small numbers are easy to say yes to without a real choice.

Multiply by twelve

One evening, she tries a simple idea. She writes each payment on a list and multiplies it by twelve. The design tool, which is billed at 15 dollars a month, becomes 180 dollars a year. When the costs add up on paper, the list looks very different.

Then she asks a second question: what else could that yearly amount do? For her, 180 dollars is close to the price of an online course she has wanted to take. Now the choice is clear. It is not "15 dollars or nothing." It is "this tool or that course."

Keep what you value

This does not mean she must cancel everything. She still uses the note app every day, so she keeps it happily. The design tool is different. She opened it only twice last month, so she decides it is not worth it for now. She can sign up again later if a project needs it.

The yearly number is a tool for comparing, not a rule. A payment can be large and still be a good choice, and it can be small and still be a waste. Unless you see the full cost, though, it is hard to compare it with the goals you care about.

A small step for tonight

Pick one monthly payment. Multiply it by twelve, and next to the number write one thing you could do with that money in a year. Then ask yourself honestly which one you would choose. You do not have to cut anything today. Seeing the yearly number simply lets you keep what you value and drop what you do not.

Words and phrases to notice

add up
become a large amount when put together. "Small costs add up."
worth it
good enough to pay for or spend time on
is charged
money is taken for something you pay for
is billed
you are asked to pay a set amount regularly
cancel
stop a service or plan you pay for

English to notice

Passive voice: “Each one is charged to her card automatically.” The passive (is + past participle) puts the focus on the payment, not on the company that takes the money.

Unless: “Unless you see the full cost, it is hard to compare it with the goals you care about.” Unless means “if not”: if you do not see the full cost, comparing is hard.

Think about it

Think of one thing you pay for every month. How much does it add up to in a year, and is it worth it? Try using “unless” in your answer.
