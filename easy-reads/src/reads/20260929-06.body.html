A girl who loved math

Ada Lovelace was born in London in 1815. Her father was Lord Byron, a famous poet. He left the family when she was a baby, and she never really knew him.

Her mother wanted Ada to study math and science. So Ada had teachers from a young age. She liked math very much. She also had a strong imagination.

A friend with big machines

When Ada was a teenager, she met Charles Babbage. Babbage was an inventor, a person who makes new things. He was working on machines that could do math.

His biggest idea was the Analytical Engine. It was a large machine made of metal parts. People could give it orders on cards with holes in them. It could store numbers and use them later. In many ways, it was like a computer. But it ran on gears, not on electricity.

Babbage never finished it. It was too big and cost too much. The machine was never built.

The notes

An Italian engineer wrote a paper about the engine in French. In 1843, Ada put it into English. She also added her own notes. Her notes were longer than the paper itself.

In one note, she wrote the steps the machine should follow to find a set of special numbers. Each step was small and clear. Today we call a list of steps like this an algorithm. Many people call it the first computer program.

More than numbers

Ada also saw something that Babbage did not stress. The machine could work with more than numbers. Numbers could stand for other things, like letters or musical notes.

She wrote that such an engine might one day make music. This was more than a hundred years before real computers. Today, computers work with text, pictures and sound. All of it is stored as numbers.

Ada died in 1852. She was only 36. Many years later, a computer language was named Ada after her.

Words to notice

inventor
a person who makes new things
gears
wheels with teeth that turn each other
engineer
a person who designs machines or buildings
algorithm
a list of small steps to solve a problem
imagination
the power to picture new ideas in your mind
