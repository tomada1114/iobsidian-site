Too many phone calls

Rosa ran a small bakery on a quiet street. She loved baking bread, but every morning the phone kept ringing. One Tuesday, she was answering her third call in ten minutes when a tray of rolls started to burn in the oven.

That evening, her nephew Ken came by after his classes. Ken was a student who could write code, and he listened to her story. "I can build you an app," he said. "People can order online, and it can answer their questions with a chat bot."

Rosa smiled, but she looked tired. She said that she did not know what an app should do. She only knew that the phone took too much of her day.

Counting before building

Ken thought about it for a moment. "Before I build anything, let's find out which questions really come up," he said. He gave her a sheet of paper and asked her to make a tally, a quick count with small lines, each time someone called.

For one week, Rosa added a line next to each question. Some questions came up only once. But five questions came up again and again: What time do you open? Do you have bread without eggs? Can I order a birthday cake? How many days ahead should I order? Is there parking nearby?

When Ken looked at the paper, he was surprised. Those five questions covered most of the calls. Nobody had asked for a chat bot, and most people only wanted the same simple facts.

A simple page

So Ken did not build an app. Instead, he made one plain web page with the five answers. Rosa printed the same answers on a small card and put it next to the cash register. She also added the page address to her shop sign.

The phone did not stop ringing. Some people still wanted to talk, and some questions were new. But a month later, Rosa said that she got far fewer calls about opening times and cakes. She could finish a tray of bread without running to the phone.

"Your page made a real difference," she told Ken. He laughed. He said that the useful part was not his code. It was her sheet of paper with the little lines.

What Ken learned

Ken wanted to build something big, but the problem was smaller than he thought. Counting the questions first showed him where a simple fix would help most. If you can build tools, it is easy to start with the tool. It often helps more to start by counting.

Words and phrases to notice

come up
appear or be asked, often without planning
make a difference
have a clear, good effect on something
tally
a quick count, often made with small lines
nephew
the son of your brother or sister
cash register
the machine in a shop where you pay

English to notice

Was answering ... when: “She was answering her third call in ten minutes when a tray of rolls started to burn.” The longer action was in progress, and a short event happened in the middle of it.

She said that ...: “Rosa said that she got far fewer calls.” We use this to report someone’s words without quoting them exactly. The verb often moves into the past.

Think about it

For one week, keep a tally of the questions people ask you most often. Which questions do you think will come up most? Try using “come up” or “make a difference” in your answer.
