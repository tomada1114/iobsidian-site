Afraid to ask

Mei was a junior developer on a small team. She liked her work, but she was afraid to ask questions in the team chat. She worried that her questions sounded silly, so she often waited for hours before she asked anything.

One afternoon, she was fixing a login page when the tests suddenly started to fail. She tried to figure out the problem, which means to understand it by thinking it through, but nothing worked. By five o'clock, she was still stuck and very tired.

Finally, she wrote a short message: "The login tests are failing. Can someone help?" Then she closed her laptop and felt bad about it all evening.

A long conversation

The next morning, a senior teammate, Daniel, answered. He asked which tests were failing. Mei sent the names. He asked if she had changed anything. She said yes, and explained. He asked for the error message, so she copied it into the chat.

The answer came after almost an hour of messages back and forth. The fix itself was small. Mei thanked him, but she felt that she had taken too much of his time.

Two lines first

Later that week, Daniel sent her a friendly message. He said that her questions were welcome. He also said that he could help faster if she wrote two things first: what she had tried, and what happened.

The next time Mei got stuck on a problem, she tried it. Before her question, she wrote, "I tried clearing the cache. The same error still appears." It took her only a minute, and sometimes, while she was writing those lines, she noticed the answer herself.

This time, Daniel replied within ten minutes. Because he could see her steps, he did not need to ask the same questions again. He explained why clearing the cache did not help, and Mei understood the reason, not just the fix.

Still asking, but calmer

Mei still asks questions, and she still feels a little nervous sometimes. But her questions now feel like the start of a short conversation, not a request for rescue. She learns more from each answer, because she can compare it with what she tried. Next time you get stuck, you could write two lines too: what you tried and what happened.

Words and phrases to notice

figure out
understand or solve something by thinking about it
got stuck on
could not move forward with something
junior developer
a developer with little work experience
back and forth
going from one person to the other many times
rescue
saving someone from a difficult situation

English to notice

Was fixing ... when: “She was fixing a login page when the tests suddenly started to fail.” The longer action was in progress, and a short event happened in the middle of it.

He said that ...: “He said that her questions were welcome.” We use this to report someone’s words without quoting them. The verb usually moves into the past.

Think about it

Think of a time you got stuck on a problem at work or in study. What did you try, and what happened? Try using “get stuck on” or “figure out” in your answer.
