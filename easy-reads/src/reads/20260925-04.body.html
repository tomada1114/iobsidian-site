Tears in the kitchen

You are cutting an onion for dinner. After a short time, your eyes sting and tears begin to fall. You are not sad. Something from the onion has reached your eyes.

This happens because cutting changes the onion’s cells. A cell is a tiny part that makes up a plant or animal. Inside a whole onion, some substances stay apart from one another.

When cells open

A knife breaks open many cells at once. The substances that were kept apart can now mix. This starts a chemical reaction, a change that makes something new.

The reaction makes an irritating chemical that can move through the air as a vapor. Your face is close to the onion, so the vapor can reach your eyes. That is why chopping the onion can make you cry, even when you feel calm.

Cutting can also send tiny drops of onion material into the air. The irritation can start while the onion is still on the board, before it goes into a pan.

Why your eyes make tears

The surface of your eye is wet. When the irritating material reaches it, you may feel a sting, a small, sharp pain. Your body reacts by making extra tears.

The tears try to wash away the irritant. This is one way your eyes protect themselves, much like they do when dust gets in. The tears are not a sign that something is badly wrong; they help clear the surface.

A gentler cut

A 2025 study tested how tiny droplets fly when onions are cut. In the tests, a blunt blade sent more droplets with more energy than a sharp one. Faster cuts also sent more droplets.

The researchers measured droplets, not how much people cried. A sharp knife and a controlled pace may reduce spray, but they cannot promise dry eyes. You may still feel a sting. Be careful with the knife, and take your time.

Words to notice

cell
a tiny part that makes up a plant or animal
chemical reaction
a change that makes something new
keep apart
make sure two things do not touch or mix
vapor
the gas form of a substance
sting
give a small, sharp pain
wash away
remove something by using water or another liquid

English to notice

When the irritating material reaches it, you may feel a sting, a small, sharp pain.

The when-clause tells when. “May” shows this is possible, not certain.
