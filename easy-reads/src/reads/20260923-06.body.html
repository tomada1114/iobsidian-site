A curious girl

Grace Hopper was born in New York in 1906. As a child, she loved to know how things worked. One day she took apart a clock to look inside. Then she took apart more clocks.

She studied math in college. Later she became a math teacher. She liked to explain hard ideas in simple ways. This skill helped her all her life.

Working with early computers

During World War Two, Hopper joined the US Navy. The Navy sent her to work on one of the first computers. It was very big. It filled a whole room.

At that time, people talked to computers only with numbers. Every order was a long line of numbers and codes. This work was slow. It was also easy to make mistakes.

There is a famous story from those years. Her team found a real moth, a small flying insect, inside a machine. They wrote "first actual case of bug being found" in their notebook. The word "bug" was already in use, but the story made it famous.

Words, not only numbers

Hopper had a new idea. Computers could understand words, not only numbers. People could write orders in something close to English. Then a program could change those words into numbers for the machine.

Many people did not believe her. They said computers could only do math. But she did not give up. Her team built a compiler, a program that turns human-like code into machine code.

Her team made a language with English words like "ADD" and "COMPARE". Later, this work helped create COBOL, a language for business. Banks and offices used it to handle money and records. Some old systems still run COBOL today.

A long career

Hopper stayed in the Navy for a very long time. She left and came back more than once. In the end, she became an admiral, a very high officer in the Navy.

She also liked to teach young people. She often gave them a short piece of wire. It showed how far light goes in a very small part of a second. She wanted people to see why time matters in computers.

Grace Hopper died in 1992. Today, when we write code with words, we use her idea.
