A curious girl

Grace Hopper was born in New York in 1906. As a child, she loved to know how things worked. One day she took apart a clock to look inside. Then she took apart more clocks.

She studied math in college. Later she became a math teacher. She liked to explain hard ideas in simple ways. This skill helped her all her life.

Working with early computers

During World War Two, Hopper joined the US Navy. The Navy sent her to work on one of the first computers. It was very big. It filled a whole room.

Early programming used detailed numbers and codes that were hard to check. A small error could change what a machine did. Writing a program was therefore only part of the job; people also needed ways to read, reuse and check their instructions.

There is a famous story from her later computer work. Her team found a real moth, a small flying insect, inside a machine. They wrote "first actual case of bug being found" in their notebook. The word "bug" was already in use, but the story made it famous.

Words, not only numbers

Hopper worked on ways to make programs easier for people to read. Programmers could write instructions in a clearer form. Another program could turn them into machine instructions. The computer still followed precise rules; it did not understand ordinary English like a person.

Many people did not believe her. They said computers could only do math. But she did not give up. Her team built a compiler, a program that turns human-like code into machine code.

Her team made a language with English words like "ADD" and "COMPARE". Later, this work helped create COBOL, a language for business. Banks and offices used it to handle money and records. Some old systems still run COBOL today.

A long career

Hopper stayed in the Navy for a very long time. She left and came back more than once. In the end, she became an admiral, a very high officer in the Navy.

She also liked to teach young people. She often gave them a short piece of wire. It showed how far light goes in a very small part of a second. She wanted people to see why time matters in computers.

Grace Hopper died in 1992. Her work helped make readable programming languages useful for business. Many people contributed to that change. Her teaching offers a small lesson too: choose one hard idea and find a clear example that another person can follow.

Words to notice

take apart
to separate something into its small pieces
moth
a small flying insect, like a butterfly
compiler
a program that turns human-like code into machine code
admiral
a very high officer in the Navy
give up
to stop trying

English to notice

Could for past ability: “Programmers could write instructions in a clearer form.” Could describes an ability provided by the tools used in that earlier work.

Think about it

Think of a tool you learned to use in the past. Use could to describe something you were able to do after learning it.
