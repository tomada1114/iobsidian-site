Why do small costs hide?

Many of us pay for music, cloud storage and a few online tools every month. Each charge feels small, and it is taken from our card automatically, so we rarely look at it. That is exactly why these costs are easy to forget.

A subscription, a service you pay for again and again, is easy to start and easy to keep. You sign up once for a good reason. Months later, the reason may be gone, but the payment is still there.

What question should I ask?

You do not need a long budget plan. Ask one question of each charge: "Did I use this last month?" The answer is usually clear, and it separates the services that help you from the ones that are only a habit.

Here is a simple rule. If you used it, you will keep it. If you did not, you will pause or cancel it, and then you will check again next month. A paused service is not lost; you can start it again when you really need it.

What about tools that overlap?

Imagine someone who pays for two note apps. Both store notes and sync across devices, but only one was opened last month. The other is a tool that does the same job, so it adds cost without adding value. Keeping the one that gets used is an easy choice.

Sometimes the answer is less simple. A tool you use only once a year, like one that helps with taxes, may still be worth it. In that case, ask a second question: "Would I pay for this again today?" If the answer is yes, keep it without guilt.

How do I start?

Open your card statement and list every monthly charge. Next to each one, write "used" or "not used" for last month. When you add up the "not used" charges, you may be surprised by the total.

This review takes a short time, and it does not ask you to give up things you enjoy. It only moves money away from the services you forgot and toward something that matters more to you, like a course, a trip or simple savings.

Words and phrases to notice

subscription
a service you pay for again and again
add up
find the total of several numbers: "Add up your charges."
be worth it
be good enough for the money: "The course was worth it."
statement
a list of payments from your bank or card
overlap
do some of the same things

English to notice

If you ..., you will ...: “If you used it, you will keep it.” This first conditional shows a real result that follows a real condition.

The ones that ...: “the services that help you from the ones that are only a habit.” The relative clause “that ...” tells us which ones we mean.

Think about it

Which of your monthly services are the ones that you really use? Try answering with “If I ..., I will ...” and say whether one is worth it.
