A man and his word lists

About a hundred and fifty years ago, a German man named Hermann Ebbinghaus wanted to know how memory works. He had no lab and no team. So he used the only mind he had: his own.

He made long lists of short, fake words, like "dax" or "bok." The words had no meaning. That was the point. He did not want old memories to help him. He learned each list, and then he tested himself later.

We forget fast at first

Ebbinghaus found something simple. We forget most things very fast at first. After one day, a large part of a new list was gone. After that, the loss was slower.

He drew this on paper as a line that drops quickly and then goes flat. Today we call it the forgetting curve. The line falls hard in the first hours. Then it slowly bends and levels out.

This is true for many kinds of learning. New words, new names and new ideas all fade in the same way.

Each review makes it last

Ebbinghaus also found good news. When he looked at a list again, he learned it faster than the first time. And after each review, he forgot it more slowly.

So the best plan is to review just before you forget. Look at new words after one day. Then look again after three days, then a week, then a month. Each gap is wider than the last one. This is called the spacing effect.

Studying all night is different. It puts a lot into your head at once. You may pass the test the next morning. But a week later, most of it is gone, because you never came back to it.

How to use it

You do not need more time. You need the same time, cut into small parts. Twenty minutes on five days is better than two hours on one night.

Many study apps use this idea. They show you a card again just when you start to forget it. Cards you know well come back less often. Hard cards come back soon. Software engineers may see this as a kind of queue, sorted by the next review date.
