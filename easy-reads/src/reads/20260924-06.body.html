A number lenders use

In the US, many people have a credit score. Banks use this number to estimate how likely someone is to pay a loan back on time. The number comes from credit records. It does not measure whether you are a good person.

Many scores range from 300 to 850. A higher score can make borrowing easier. A lower score may reflect late payments, high debt or a short history. It does not explain the whole situation.

This number matters in daily life. You may need it to get a credit card or a car loan, money you borrow and pay back later. Some landlords use credit information when checking a person's application to rent a home.

Built from your history

So where does the number come from? It comes from reported credit history. Lenders can keep records of borrowing and payments. Which records are reported matters for what appears in your credit report.

A lender may send those records to companies called credit bureaus. Bureaus collect reported information, which a scoring model can use. Borrowing that is not reported cannot contribute to that model in the same way.

Paying on time matters, but other things matter too, such as how much you owe and how long you have borrowed. A payment does not always make your score rise. Different models can also give you different scores.

When records are missing

Many new arrivals have too little US credit history for a score. Having no score is different from having a low one. Some newcomers already have enough records, so their situation is different.

A good payment history in another country may not appear in your US credit report. A lender may have other ways to assess an application. But the usual US score needs information from the records it uses.

Think of a new user account in an app. It may have little data, even when the person using it has years of experience. Missing records are not proof of bad behavior.

Making payments visible

One type of card is a secured card. You leave money with the bank to protect it if you do not pay. The spending limit often matches that amount, but terms and fees vary. The deposit does not replace paying the bill.

If the lender reports your payments, the records can help build a history. How soon a score appears depends on the model and the records. The useful question is what gets reported, rather than a promise of a score after a fixed number of months.
