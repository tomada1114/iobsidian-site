Why DynamoDB and not PostgreSQL?

After this page you can choose a database from decision drivers instead of habit. You can also say what DynamoDB costs, and when a relational database is the better pick.

About 8 min readAfter the design review

In this page

  • What should a database choice start from?
  • How did the options compare?
  • What does DynamoDB cost, and when does relational win?
  • How do you defend the choice in a review?

After the Tuesday design review. A teammate said the team would have shipped faster with Postgres.

01Drivers before products

L

Leo: That comment in the review stuck with me. "We'd have shipped faster with Postgres." I half agree. Why didn't we pick it?

M

Maya: Which half do you agree with?

L

Leo: PostgreSQL is the safe default. Everyone knows SQL. NoSQL is for companies with huge scale, and we're small.

M

Maya: That's how most teams choose: from habit, SQL or NoSQL first. We did it the other way round. We wrote down the decision drivers first.

L

Leo: Decision drivers?

M

Maya: The things the choice must be good at, written before looking at any product. Then every option is judged against the same list.

L

Leo: What was on our list?

M

Maya: First, a boundary per learner that holds by structure. One learner's data should be separate because of how it's stored, not because every query remembers a filter.

L

Leo: Second?

M

Maya: Deleting and exporting one learner's data easily. Third, no cost when idle. Fourth, no VPC and no connection management from Lambda.

L

Leo: Connection management meaning a pooler, because many Lambdas would each open connections?

M

Maya: Exactly that. Fifth, conditional writes, so resent answers are harmless. And sixth, a history complete enough to rebuild derived data, like stats.

L

Leo: None of those say "SQL" or "NoSQL".

M

Maya: That's the point. They describe what the app needs. Here's the process as a whole.

02The options side by side

L

Leo: So which options were on the list?

M

Maya: Four. DynamoDB on-demand. Aurora DSQL, a serverless database that speaks PostgreSQL. Aurora Serverless v2, which is full PostgreSQL. And classic RDS PostgreSQL.

L

Leo: Start with RDS. That's the usual one.

M

Maya: An instance billed every hour, used or not. It lives in a VPC. And many Lambdas need a connection pooler in front of it.

L

Leo: So it fails idle cost and the no-VPC driver right away.

M

Maya: Right. Aurora Serverless v2 can scale down to zero on some versions. But it's unclear whether Lambda can reach it without a VPC. If not, NAT costs come back.

L

Leo: And DSQL?

M

Maya: DSQL was the real competitor. It's serverless, it speaks PostgreSQL, and an idle cluster costs nothing. It bills per unit of work.

L

Leo: That sounds almost perfect. What was wrong with it?

M

Maya: Its transactions have limits. There's one isolation level, a cap on how many rows one transaction may change, and no savepoints.

L

Leo: What's a savepoint?

M

Maya: A marker inside a transaction you can roll back to, without undoing the whole thing. And DSQL uses optimistic concurrency, so a commit can fail and must be retried from the start.

L

Leo: And local development?

M

Maya: We'd run plain PostgreSQL on our laptops, a different engine from production. Tests could pass locally and fail in the cloud. Let me compare the two finalists.

L

Leo: So in DSQL, every query would need WHERE learner_id = ?, and one missing filter leaks data.

M

Maya: That's the risk. In our table, a learner's data is one partition. Reading, deleting and exporting all start from one key.

03What DynamoDB costs

L

Leo: Okay, but the teammate wasn't completely wrong, right?

M

Maya: Partly right. Let's be honest about what DynamoDB costs. Every new read must be designed as a key pattern first.

L

Leo: With SQL, I'd just write a new query.

M

Maya: Yes. Reporting needs an export to S3, not a query on the live table. And derived data, like stats, can drift from the answer log.

L

Leo: Drift, meaning the stats say something different from the answers?

M

Maya: If a bug updates one and not the other, yes. That's why the history must be complete enough to rebuild the stats.

L

Leo: So when does relational win?

M

Maya: When access patterns are still unknown. When data has many-to-many relations and constraints worth enforcing.

L

Leo: Many-to-many, like cards and grammar points? One card trains several points, and one point has many cards.

M

Maya: That's a good example of the shape. In our case we store the tags on the card, and we never ask the reverse question live. If we did, a relational database would handle it with a join.

L

Leo: Any other cases?

M

Maya: When the business lives on ad hoc reports. And when the team's skills make the difference: a team fluent in SQL moves faster with it.

L

Leo: And for a quick prototype?

M

Maya: For a CRUD prototype, Postgres is often faster to build. Your teammate is right about that. Our early prototype kept progress in a local SQL file on one machine.

L

Leo: So what changed?

M

Maya: Multi-learner sign-in. The moment many learners shared one system, the drivers changed, and so did the database. Here's the choice in two questions.

04Both, and how to defend it

L

Leo: Do teams ever use both?

M

Maya: Many SaaS teams do. A relational database for the core records and reporting, and a key-value store for a hot path, like sessions or counters.

L

Leo: That sounds like the best of both.

M

Maya: It's also two systems to operate, and two copies of some data to keep in sync. You pay for it every day.

L

Leo: So how do I say all this in a review, without sounding defensive?

M

Maya: Three moves. Name the drivers. Show each option against them. And write down what would make you revisit.

L

Leo: What's our revisit condition?

M

Maya: If exports make it convenient, we move the answer log to its own table. It's written in the decision record, so nobody has to remember it.

L

Leo: And if the revisit condition never comes true?

M

Maya: Then the record says why we're still here. A new teammate reads it and doesn't have to reopen the whole debate.

L

Leo: So the teammate's comment isn't an attack. It's a chance to check the drivers again.

M

Maya: Yes. If a driver changed, the choice may change too. If not, the answer is already written down.

05Leo explains it back

M

Maya: So what do you tell your teammate tomorrow?

L

Leo: We didn't start from "SQL or NoSQL". We listed what mattered first.

L

Leo: A learner boundary that's structural, per-learner delete and export, nothing billed while idle, and no connections to manage from Lambda.

L

Leo: DynamoDB fit those best. DSQL was close, but it brought transaction limits and a different engine for local development.

M

Maya: And the price?

L

Leo: Every new question needs a key design, and reports come from an export.

M

Maya: Finish with when you'd switch. If our questions were still unknown, or we needed joins and reports, PostgreSQL would be the right pick. Saying that makes the rest believable.

Summary in three lines

  1. Choose a database from written decision drivers, and judge every option against the same list.
  2. DynamoDB won on a structural learner boundary; its price is designing every question as a key first.
  3. Pick relational when questions are unknown or relations are rich, and write down what would make you revisit.
