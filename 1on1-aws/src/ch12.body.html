Where should a web app keep its tokens?

After this page you can explain why tokens stay out of page JavaScript, and what each cookie attribute protects. You can also explain how CSRF defenses and refresh-token rotation fit in.

About 8 min readOn a video call

In this page

  • What's the risk of keeping tokens in localStorage?
  • What do the backend and its cookies do?
  • Why do cookies need CSRF defenses?
  • What does this design cost?

A video call. Leo has a tutorial open in another tab.

01The risk in localStorage

L

Leo: I read a tutorial that keeps the access token in localStorage. The page sends it as a Bearer header. It's about ten lines.

M

Maya: And ours?

L

Leo: Four auth endpoints on the API, three cookies, and Origin checks. Why didn't we do the simple thing?

M

Maya: What can read localStorage?

L

Leo: Our own JavaScript.

M

Maya: Any JavaScript running on the page. Ours, and anything that gets in.

L

Leo: But we have no XSS bug. We escape everything.

M

Maya: An XSS bug, cross-site scripting, lets an attacker run their script on our page. You're sure there are none today.

M

Maya: Today. And our dependencies? One compromised npm package runs with the same rights as our code.

L

Leo: So it reads localStorage and sends the tokens somewhere else.

M

Maya: Including the refresh token. That one gives access for weeks, not minutes.

L

Leo: And a cookie is different how?

M

Maya: An HttpOnly cookie can't be read by scripts at all. The browser attaches it to requests by itself.

L

Leo: So an XSS bug is still bad with cookies.

M

Maya: Still bad. But the attacker can only act while the page is open. They can't carry the token away and use it for weeks.

02The backend and its cookies

L

Leo: If scripts can't touch the tokens, who does the sign-in?

M

Maya: Our API, acting as a small backend-for-frontend. It hosts login, callback, refresh and logout, and keeps the client secret on the server.

L

Leo: And it sets the cookies.

M

Maya: Three of them. The access token, the refresh token, and a short-lived one holding the sign-in state and the PKCE verifier.

L

Leo: How long does each live?

M

Maya: The access cookie lives as long as the token. The refresh cookie lasts 30 days. The sign-in state lasts ten minutes.

L

Leo: And the attributes? I always forget what each one does.

M

Maya: Let me put the access cookie in the middle.

L

Leo: What does the __Host- prefix add?

M

Maya: The browser refuses the cookie unless it's Secure, on Path=/, and has no Domain. So no sibling subdomain can plant one for us.

L

Leo: Why would anyone plant a cookie?

M

Maya: To sign you in as the attacker. Then everything you practice lands in their account, where they can read it.

L

Leo: And the refresh token, if it leaks anyway?

M

Maya: Rotation. Each refresh returns a new refresh token, and the old one stops working after a ten-second grace period. Logout revokes it.

L

Leo: Why a grace period at all?

M

Maya: Two tabs may refresh at the same moment. Without it, one of them fails for no reason.

L

Leo: What happens when the access token expires mid-session?

M

Maya: This loop. The learner never sees it.

L

Leo: Why does the refresh itself need an Origin check?

M

Maya: It's a POST that changes state: it swaps tokens. Logout is the same. Another site shouldn't be able to trigger either one.

L

Leo: And if the refresh token has expired too?

M

Maya: Then the refresh fails, and the page sends the browser to sign in. The learner types their password once a month at most.

L

Leo: And all this works because the API and the app share an address?

M

Maya: Yes. CloudFront puts both on one origin, so the cookies reach the API.

03Forged requests

L

Leo: You said cookies need a CSRF defense. Isn't CSRF a solved problem?

M

Maya: Only if you add the defenses yourself. The browser sends our cookie automatically, even when another site starts the request.

L

Leo: So an evil page could post a form to our API, and my cookie goes along.

M

Maya: That's the attack. Picture a page that quietly posts "reset my progress" to our API. The learner visits it, and their history is gone.

L

Leo: Without ever touching our site.

M

Maya: Right. We have three defenses.

L

Leo: SameSite=Lax is one.

M

Maya: Right. With Lax, the cookie isn't sent on cross-site POSTs. Second, every state-changing request has its Origin header checked against an allowlist.

L

Leo: Isn't SameSite enough on its own?

M

Maya: Not quite. "Same site" includes sibling subdomains, and some older browsers ignore the attribute. The Origin check doesn't depend on either.

L

Leo: Why not check Origin on reads too?

M

Maya: Reads change nothing, so a forged read gains nothing. The attacker's page can't see the response anyway.

L

Leo: And the third?

M

Maya: The callback. It's a navigation from Cognito's page, so it carries no Origin we could check.

L

Leo: So we compare the state value instead.

M

Maya: In constant time, so the comparison doesn't leak how many characters matched.

L

Leo: And the phone apps' Bearer path?

M

Maya: No CSRF check needed. Nothing is attached automatically, so another site can't borrow it.

04What it costs

L

Leo: This is clearly safer. What did it cost?

M

Maya: The API now owns the login flow, the cookies and the refresh timing. And the cookie path and the Bearer path must behave the same, so we test both.

L

Leo: Anything that surprised you?

M

Maya: Cookies hold about 4 KB. A large token might not fit.

L

Leo: And locally?

M

Maya: Safari won't keep Secure cookies over plain http on 127.0.0.1. So local sign-in uses another browser.

L

Leo: What if we moved the API to its own domain one day?

M

Maya: This setup breaks. The cookies belong to one origin.

L

Leo: Why Lax and not Strict? Strict sounds safer.

M

Maya: Strict drops the cookie on every arrival from another site. A learner following a link from their email would look signed out.

L

Leo: And the redirect back from Cognito is from another site too.

M

Maya: Right, so Strict would break the sign-in itself. Lax sends the cookie on those top-level navigations, but not on cross-site POSTs.

L

Leo: Is localStorage ever acceptable?

M

Maya: For a low-risk app, a token kept only in memory, short-lived, can be fine. Native apps use Bearer tokens in the system's secure storage.

L

Leo: I was sure the ten-line version was just the modern way.

M

Maya: It's shorter because it leaves the risk with the learner. We chose to carry it on the server.

05Leo explains it back

M

Maya: Someone posts that tutorial in our channel. What do you reply?

L

Leo: If a token sits in localStorage, any script on the page can steal it. That includes a compromised dependency.

L

Leo: So our API does the sign-in exchange and puts the tokens in HttpOnly cookies with the __Host- prefix. Scripts can't read them, and other hosts can't set them.

L

Leo: The browser sends them automatically, so we add CSRF defenses. That means SameSite=Lax, an Origin check on every change, and the state value on the callback.

M

Maya: And the refresh token?

L

Leo: It rotates on every use, and logout revokes it.

M

Maya: It all rests on one thing: CloudFront serves the app and the API from one origin.

Summary in three lines

  1. Tokens in localStorage can be read by any script on the page, including a compromised dependency.
  2. A backend-for-frontend keeps tokens in HttpOnly, Secure, SameSite cookies with the __Host- prefix.
  3. Cookies are sent automatically, so add CSRF defenses and rotate the refresh token.
