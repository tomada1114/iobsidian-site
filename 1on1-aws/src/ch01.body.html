What am I actually responsible for on AWS?

After this page you can explain regions, accounts and the shared-responsibility line. You can also say why production gets its own account, and why pricing is part of the design.

In this page

  • Why separate accounts for dev and prod?
  • Why does the way a service is priced matter?

The office kitchen, in the morning. Leo got his own sign-in to the dev account an hour ago.

01Regions and accounts

L

Leo: I'm in the dev account now. Can I ask two basic questions?

M

Maya: Basic questions are the best ones. Go.

L

Leo: First: we're "on AWS", so AWS takes care of security, right? Second: why do we have separate dev and prod accounts? One account would work fine.

M

Maya: Both answers come from the same map. When you create something on AWS, where does it live?

L

Leo: In our account. And in Tokyo, I think.

M

Maya: Both right. Tokyo is a region, a geographic area. Each region is made of several availability zones. Those are separate data centers, far enough apart that one fire or flood doesn't take down two.

L

Leo: So when I create a Lambda function, it lives in one region.

M

Maya: Yes, most services are regional. A few are global, like IAM, which controls who can do what, and CloudFront, the content delivery network. Everything of ours runs in the Tokyo region, because our first learners are in Japan.

L

Leo: And the account? I thought it was just a login.

M

Maya: That's the common view. An account is a container. It holds resources, the permissions on those resources, and their bill.

L

Leo: A container for resources, permissions and money.

M

Maya: And damage, as we'll see. Let me draw how they fit.

02Shared responsibility

L

Leo: Okay, then the first question. Security is AWS's job, isn't it?

M

Maya: Half of it. AWS calls this the shared responsibility model. AWS secures the cloud itself: buildings, hardware, and the managed service. You secure what you put in it.

L

Leo: What counts as "what I put in it"?

M

Maya: Identities and permissions, data, configuration, and code. If a Lambda function has a role that can read every table, AWS won't stop it.

L

Leo: So a bad permission is our bug, not AWS's.

M

Maya: Right. And the line moves with how managed a service is. Let me draw it for two kinds of compute.

L

Leo: So on a virtual machine I'd also patch the operating system.

M

Maya: Yes. On Lambda and DynamoDB, AWS does the patching. But IAM and data are always yours. No service takes those away from you.

L

Leo: That's a relief and a worry at the same time.

M

Maya: Less to patch, yes. But the part that's left is the part attackers aim for.

03The account as a blast radius

L

Leo: Back to the second question, then. Why two accounts?

M

Maya: Think of an account as a blast radius, the area a mistake can damage. What happens if someone deletes a table by accident in dev?

L

Leo: We lose test data. Annoying, not serious.

M

Maya: And if dev and prod were one account, with one set of permissions?

L

Leo: Then the same mistake could hit learners' data. Or a leaked dev key could reach prod.

M

Maya: Right. A mistake, a leaked credential, or a runaway bill stays inside one account. So production usually gets its own.

L

Leo: And the organization is the thing around both accounts?

M

Maya: Yes. We use AWS Organizations. There's a management account that holds only billing and identity, plus dev and prod as member accounts.

L

Leo: Why keep workloads out of the management account?

M

Maya: The organization's guard policies, which limit what any account may do, don't apply to the management account. So we keep it nearly empty.

L

Leo: And how do people sign in?

M

Maya: Through IAM Identity Center, one sign-in for both accounts. Each account's root user, the all-powerful first user, has MFA and no access keys.

L

Leo: What does all this cost?

M

Maya: Organizations and Identity Center have no extra charge. The real cost is setup and more to operate. And one surprise: creating an organization ends a new account's free-plan credits at once.

L

Leo: So you paid for it on day one.

M

Maya: We did, on purpose. Prod holds learners' data, and nothing done in dev can reach it. For a weekend project I'd skip it.

L

Leo: Couldn't one account do the same job with careful IAM roles? A dev role that can't touch prod tables?

M

Maya: It can, if everything goes right. But every new resource needs the right tags and the right policy, and one missed line lets dev reach prod.

L

Leo: So the separation depends on everyone getting every policy right.

M

Maya: Yes. With two accounts, the separation is the default. A dev role simply has no way into prod, even if its policy is too wide.

L

Leo: And the bill is split for free, too. I can see what dev costs.

M

Maya: Right. And when a budget alarm fires, you know which account to look at first.

04Pricing is part of the design

L

Leo: You said "runaway bill". How do bills run away?

M

Maya: It helps to know how each part is priced. Some things bill per hour, whether anyone uses them or not.

L

Leo: Like what?

M

Maya: A virtual machine, a load balancer, a NAT gateway. Others bill per request or per gigabyte: Lambda, API Gateway, DynamoDB on-demand, S3.

L

Leo: And we chose mostly the per-request kind.

M

Maya: Mostly. Here's what that changes.

L

Leo: So an idle Quickturn costs almost nothing.

M

Maya: That's the goal. A dev account that sleeps at night should cost close to zero.

L

Leo: Is per-request always cheaper, then?

M

Maya: No. At steady, high traffic, per-hour capacity can become cheaper and easier to predict. Per request, you pay a little on every call, forever.

L

Leo: So the choice depends on the traffic shape, not on which is "modern".

M

Maya: Yes. And it's a design choice, made early. For compute, moving from one to the other later usually means changing the architecture, not just a setting.

05Leo explains it back

M

Maya: Say a new teammate gets their dev sign-in tomorrow. What do you tell them?

L

Leo: A region is where resources run; ours is Tokyo, with several availability zones inside it.

L

Leo: An account is the box that holds resources, their permissions, their bill, and the damage a mistake can do.

L

Leo: AWS secures its side of the line. Our data, IAM and configuration are always ours.

M

Maya: And the two accounts?

L

Leo: Dev and prod are separate accounts in one organization, so nothing done in dev can reach learners' data.

L

Leo: And we prefer per-request pricing, so an idle app costs almost nothing.

M

Maya: That choice has a limit. If traffic became high and steady, we'd look at per-hour capacity again.

Summary in three lines

  1. A region is where things run; an account holds resources, permissions, the bill and the damage of a mistake.
  2. AWS secures the cloud; your data, IAM and configuration stay yours, however managed the service is.
  3. Separate prod into its own account, and choose per-request pricing when idle cost matters most.
