How much will this cost if nobody uses it?

After this page you can tell parts billed by the hour from parts billed per use. You can also explain how free tiers, budgets and a budget action keep spending in check from the first day.

About 8 min readCoffee in the kitchen

In this page

  • Which parts cost money even when idle?
  • What does Quickturn avoid, and what do free tiers cover?
  • What guards spending from the first day?
  • When does saving money cost too much?

The office kitchen. A budget email arrived this morning.

01Fixed versus per use

L

Leo: I got a budget forecast email this morning. Only the team uses the app. What could even cost money?

M

Maya: Let's find out. What do you think drives the bill?

L

Leo: Traffic. And we're serverless, so no traffic means no bill.

M

Maya: Half right. Serverless parts bill per use. But some parts bill for every hour they exist, used or not.

L

Leo: Such as?

M

Maya: A NAT gateway, a load balancer, an interface endpoint. An always-on instance or container. A flat monthly plan.

L

Leo: And the per-use side?

M

Maya: Lambda, API Gateway, DynamoDB on-demand, S3. Logs are billed by volume. They charge per request, per gigabyte or per second of work.

L

Leo: So "serverless is cheap" is only true for the per-use side.

M

Maya: And only at low traffic. So I ask two questions of every design.

L

Leo: Which are?

M

Maya: What does it cost at zero traffic? And at ten times today's?

L

Leo: Draw me the two sides?

M

Maya: Two questions make four corners.

L

Leo: The top right looks like the worst corner.

M

Maya: It is for a small app. A NAT gateway alone bills about four and a half cents an hour in one example region, as of September 2026.

L

Leo: That's over thirty dollars a month, before any traffic.

M

Maya: Right. For an app only the team uses, that could be the whole bill.

02What we avoid, what's free

L

Leo: So what did we leave out on purpose?

M

Maya: No NAT gateway, no load balancer, no interface endpoints. That's also why we have no VPC.

L

Leo: And the monitoring? Alarms cost money too, don't they?

M

Maya: We sized them to CloudWatch's free tier: five alarms and one dashboard. Function logs are kept for one month.

L

Leo: I thought logs were basically free. They're just text.

M

Maya: Logs are billed by the gigabyte, both when written and while stored. A debug line on every request, kept forever, grows every month.

L

Leo: So one month of retention caps the stored part.

M

Maya: And we log one line per request, not the whole body.

L

Leo: And sign-in?

M

Maya: Cognito's free monthly users cover us. CloudFront is on its flat-rate Free plan.

L

Leo: Wait, a flat-rate plan sits in the top left. Isn't that a fixed cost?

M

Maya: It is, but the Free plan's rate is zero dollars. Fixed, and still nothing.

L

Leo: What about API Gateway?

M

Maya: It charges per request, and we accept that. If traffic ever makes it matter, a function URL behind CloudFront is the cheaper fallback.

L

Leo: So the design is deliberately boring on cost.

M

Maya: Boring is the goal. With nobody using it, the bill should be close to zero.

L

Leo: Then why did I get a forecast email?

M

Maya: A forecast alert means this month is on track to pass our budget. So something is running that shouldn't be.

L

Leo: How do I find it?

M

Maya: Open Cost Explorer and group by service. Often it's something left running after a test. First, let's look at how the guards work.

03Guards from the first day

L

Leo: When were the budgets set up?

M

Maya: Before any other resource. We created two by hand, before the first stack.

L

Leo: What are they?

M

Maya: A monthly budget of 30 dollars. It alerts at 85% and 100% of actual spend, and at 100% of forecast.

L

Leo: So my email means this month is heading past 30 dollars. And the second?

M

Maya: A zero-spend budget. It catches the first unexpected charge, however small.

L

Leo: How do we know which feature is spending?

M

Maya: Cost allocation tags. Our model calls go through inference profiles, named routes to a model that can carry tags. So AI spend shows up on its own line.

L

Leo: And an email is enough?

M

Maya: For most parts. Not for AI model calls. One bad loop can spend a month's budget overnight.

L

Leo: So what stops that?

M

Maya: A budget action. When the budget is crossed, it attaches a deny policy on its own. That's our kill switch for model calls.

L

Leo: Why may it lag?

M

Maya: Billing data takes time to arrive. By the time the action fires, more has been spent.

L

Leo: So one learner could still run up a big bill before the switch turns on.

M

Maya: That's the job of the per-learner usage ledger. It limits each learner, one call at a time.

L

Leo: How does it know the cost before the call?

M

Maya: It reserves the most a call could cost, and refunds the rest afterwards. If the learner's balance can't cover the worst case, the call doesn't happen.

L

Leo: And telemetry?

M

Maya: Each model call records its cost. So we see spend per call, per feature and per learner, not just a monthly total.

04When saving costs too much

L

Leo: So per use is always the right choice?

M

Maya: No. At steady high load, per-request pricing overtakes per-hour capacity. What's cheapest idle can be expensive at scale.

L

Leo: Give me a case.

M

Maya: Imagine the API handling many requests every second, all day. A small always-on container might then cost less than paying per request.

L

Leo: Then we'd switch when we get there.

M

Maya: Right, when the numbers say so. And engineering time is a cost too. Don't spend a week to save a few dollars a month.

L

Leo: And the kill switch? It sounds like it has no downside.

M

Maya: It trades availability for money. A false trigger turns a feature off for every learner.

L

Leo: Could a false trigger really happen?

M

Maya: A busy week of honest use could cross the budget. So could one runaway job. The switch can't tell which.

L

Leo: So one bad job can switch off the tutor for every learner in that account.

M

Maya: Yes. The alert arrives before the action, so a person usually sees it first.

L

Leo: Is this just a Quickturn habit?

M

Maya: No. AI features make each user's cost variable, and potentially unbounded. That's why teams add per-call cost telemetry, per-user limits and a hard stop for the account.

L

Leo: So cost is part of the design, not a report at the end of the month.

M

Maya: That's the whole idea.

05Leo explains it back

M

Maya: Say how you'd think about cost on your next design.

L

Leo: I ask what it costs with nobody using it, and with ten times the traffic.

L

Leo: We avoid parts that bill by the hour, like NAT gateways and load balancers. So our idle cost is close to zero, and we stay inside free tiers where we can.

L

Leo: Budgets alert us on actual and forecast spend from day one. For AI calls, a budget action is the account's kill switch, and a per-learner ledger limits each user.

M

Maya: Anything you'd watch?

L

Leo: The price of the kill switch: a false trigger turns the feature off for everyone.

M

Maya: Keep engineering time in the sum as well. A week spent saving a few dollars a month is a loss.

Summary in three lines

  1. Separate hourly parts from per-use parts, and ask what each costs at zero and at ten times the traffic.
  2. Keep idle cost near zero, and know what free tiers cover.
  3. Set budgets from day one, with a budget action and per-user limits where usage can run away.
