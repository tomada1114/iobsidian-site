Why does our deploy have no AWS keys?

After this page you can explain IAM users, roles and least privilege. You can also describe how CI gets into AWS through OIDC, with no stored secret at all.

About 8 min readPairing at Leo's desk

In this page

  • What's the difference between a user and a role?
  • How does the workflow get into AWS without a key?
  • What does least privilege cost?
  • Does an AI agent need a role too?

Leo's desk. The deploy workflow is open in the editor.

01Users and roles

L

Leo: I want to add a step to the deploy workflow. I went looking for the AWS access key in the repository secrets, and there isn't one.

M

Maya: There isn't. What did you expect to find?

L

Leo: An access key and a secret key, stored as secrets. That's how my agency did it. And admin permissions, at least in dev.

M

Maya: That's the most common setup, and it's the one we avoid. Let's start with the two kinds of identity. What's an IAM user?

L

Leo: A person's account in AWS? With a password.

M

Maya: Close. An IAM user is a long-lived identity. It can hold a password and access keys, and those keys work until someone deletes them.

L

Leo: And a role?

M

Maya: A role has no credentials of its own. Someone assumes it, and AWS returns temporary credentials that expire on their own. The service that hands them out is STS, the Security Token Service.

L

Leo: So a role is more like a hat you put on for a while.

M

Maya: That works. One more rule under all of this: everything is denied unless a policy allows it. That's called default deny.

L

Leo: What about people? How do I get into the console?

M

Maya: Through IAM Identity Center. You sign in once and get short-lived credentials for the console and the CLI. No person or agent here has a long-lived key.

L

Leo: And the root user, the one that created the account?

M

Maya: It has MFA, no access keys, and nobody uses it day to day. Let me put a user and a role side by side.

02Two sides of a role, and OIDC

L

Leo: Okay, roles. But who is allowed to put on the hat?

M

Maya: That's the first side of a role, the trust policy. It says who may assume the role. The second side, the permission policy, says what the role may do.

L

Leo: So for our workflow, the trust policy must say "GitHub Actions may assume this".

M

Maya: More precisely than that. GitHub can give a workflow a signed token that says who it is. That's OIDC, OpenID Connect, a standard way to prove identity with a signed token.

L

Leo: What does the token say?

M

Maya: Which repository, which branch, and who the token is meant for, the audience. Our trust policy checks that the audience is STS, and that the subject is exactly our repository's main branch.

L

Leo: Exactly? Not "starts with"?

M

Maya: Exactly, with a plain equals check. Let me walk through a deploy.

L

Leo: What does the workflow need for this?

M

Maya: One line of permissions: id-token: write. That lets the job ask GitHub for the token.

L

Leo: And the deploy role itself can deploy anything?

M

Maya: No. It may only assume the CDK bootstrap roles. CDK, our infrastructure tool, creates those roles once per account, and they carry the real deploy permissions.

L

Leo: So the role from GitHub is only a door to the deploy roles.

M

Maya: Right. And the prod deploy waits for a person to approve it before it starts.

L

Leo: Do running services use roles the same way?

M

Maya: Every Lambda function has an execution role. It's the same idea, used in production. Here's the API function's role.

L

Leo: What's the KMS one?

M

Maya: KMS is the key service that encrypts our secret parameter. The role may decrypt only through Parameter Store, and only for that one parameter.

L

Leo: So even a bug in the API can't read another secret.

M

Maya: Or another table. The role is the limit, whatever the code tries.

03What least privilege costs

L

Leo: This is least privilege, right? Each identity gets only what it needs.

M

Maya: Yes. And I won't pretend it's free. Every new kind of resource can mean a policy change.

L

Leo: And a policy that's too narrow breaks the deploy.

M

Maya: At the worst moment, usually. That's why wildcards are tempting: "Action": "*", and everything works.

L

Leo: I'd honestly be tempted in dev. It's just dev.

M

Maya: Where would you spend the care, then? Think about what damage each role could do.

L

Leo: Anything that reaches data. Or production.

M

Maya: Yes. And the trust policy above all. What happens if the subject is a wildcard, like "any branch of our repository"?

L

Leo: Then any branch could deploy. Someone's experiment branch could deploy to prod.

M

Maya: Worse. With a loose enough condition, a workflow in a fork of the repository could ask for the role too.

L

Leo: So one star in a trust policy lets strangers in.

M

Maya: That's the danger. Where would I relax? A personal sandbox account, with a budget alert, where nothing real lives.

L

Leo: And for our deploy?

M

Maya: We compared plain administrator access with a role that can only assume the bootstrap roles. Admin would be one line and never break.

L

Leo: But one stolen token could do anything.

M

Maya: Right. We chose the narrow role and accepted the occasional policy change.

04Agents get roles too

L

Leo: Does this matter for the tutor agent?

M

Maya: Very much. An agent hosted on a managed agent runtime, like ours on AgentCore, runs with an execution role too.

L

Leo: What goes in that role?

M

Maya: Only the model it calls and the tools it needs. Nothing for "just in case".

L

Leo: Then the role protects learners' data too?

M

Maya: Here teams often get confused. The role decides which AWS resources the agent's code can touch. It doesn't decide whose data a tool may read.

L

Leo: Because every learner's data is in the same table.

M

Maya: Exactly. The role must allow the table. Which learner's rows a tool reads is decided by our application's own authorization, from the signed-in learner.

L

Leo: So two layers. The role limits resources; the app limits whose data.

M

Maya: That's the split our tutor follows. If you ask the role to do the second job, it can't.

05Leo explains it back

M

Maya: Suppose someone asks you in review why there's no AWS secret in the repository. What do you say?

L

Leo: Users are long-lived identities with keys that work until deleted. Roles give out temporary credentials to whoever their trust policy admits.

L

Leo: Our CI proves who it is with a signed OIDC token from GitHub. STS checks the audience and that it's exactly our main branch.

L

Leo: So there's no key to leak or rotate. And the role it gets can only hand off to the CDK deploy roles.

M

Maya: And the workloads?

L

Leo: Each has its own role that lists exactly what it touches, like the API's table, one parameter, and its logs.

M

Maya: Say which part to review hardest: the trust policy. One loose condition there lets the wrong branch, or a fork, deploy.

Summary in three lines

  1. Prefer roles with temporary credentials over users with long-lived keys, and give each identity only what it needs.
  2. A trust policy says who may assume a role; OIDC lets CI prove its identity with no stored secret.
  3. Spend the most care on trust policies and anything that reaches data; a role limits resources, not whose data.
