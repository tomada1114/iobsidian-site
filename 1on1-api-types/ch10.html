Can an app work offline if the server owns the rules?

After this page you can explain offline support where the client records facts and the server applies the rules. You can also say how to replay those facts safely and which times to trust.

About 8 min readWhiteboard room

In this page

  • Does offline mean running the rules on the phone?
  • How are queued answers replayed safely?
  • Which time do we believe?
  • What does this design give up?

The small meeting room. Leo has just come back from practicing on a train.

01Two ways to be offline

L

Leo: Something surprised me on the train. I lost signal in the middle of a round, kept answering, and reloaded when the signal came back. Every answer was there.

M

Maya: What surprised you about it?

L

Leo: The phone can't compute the schedule. The server does that. So how did it work?

M

Maya: Go on. What's the worry behind the question?

L

Leo: Whether the native apps need a copy of the rules, to work offline.

M

Maya: What do you think "offline-first" usually means?

L

Leo: A local database on the phone, the rules running there, and some sync engine that fixes conflicts later.

M

Maya: That's one real design. Some apps use CRDTs for it, conflict-free replicated data types. They're data types that merge edits from two devices without conflicts.

L

Leo: That sounds hard.

M

Maya: It is. There's a second design, where the client doesn't run any rules.

L

Leo: Then what does it do?

M

Maya: It only records facts: this answer, on this card, at this time. The server decides what they mean when they arrive.

L

Leo: So the phone is just a notebook.

M

Maya: A careful notebook. Let me compare the two.

L

Leo: Four clients?

M

Maya: The web app, iOS, Android, and the tutor agent. Dealing, scheduling and streaks exist once, on the server. Nothing is copied into Swift or Kotlin.

L

Leo: But then how do I get cards offline?

M

Maya: The deck is fetched while you're online, when the round starts. Offline, you answer the cards you already have.

02The queue and the replay

L

Leo: So what happened to my answers on the train?

M

Maya: Each answer got an id the moment you gave it, made on your device. Then it went into a queue.

L

Leo: Where does the web app keep the queue?

M

Maya: In the tab's session storage, so it survives a reload. The answers go out one at a time, in order.

L

Leo: And when the send failed?

M

Maya: The failed answer stays at the head of the queue. Nothing behind it jumps ahead. When the signal came back, it was sent again with the same id.

L

Leo: What if the first send actually reached the server, and only the reply got lost?

M

Maya: Then the server already holds that id and skips it. Resending is harmless. That's why the id is made on the device, not on the server.

L

Leo: Do the native apps work the same way?

M

Maya: They keep a queue on the device that works the same way. And online and offline use the same batch endpoint. There's no special offline API.

L

Leo: Here's the whole trip, then, for one of my answers.

M

Maya: One more piece. The server applies a batch in order of time, then id. So two answers in one batch always land the same way.

L

Leo: And when I reloaded, how did the page know where I was?

M

Maya: A resume endpoint returns the round with the answers it already holds. The app continues from there.

03Time you can't trust

L

Leo: You said each answer carries a time. Which time? The phone's?

M

Maya: The phone reports when the learner answered. But can we trust a phone clock?

L

Leo: Not really. People set them by hand. They drift. And they change time zones on trips.

M

Maya: That's called clock skew. So the server clamps the reported time. Here's the whole rule.

const answeredAt = Math.min(
  Math.max(reported ?? now, round.startedAt), // not before the round began
  now,                                        // not after the server's "now"
);
L

Leo: So a time before the round started becomes the start. A time in the future becomes now.

M

Maya: And no time at all means the server's own time. Let me draw the window.

L

Leo: What about an answer that arrives days later? I had that once, with an old tab.

M

Maya: A late answer counts for the day the round started. It's logged, but it never moves a card's schedule backwards.

L

Leo: Why not?

M

Maya: Because newer answers already moved it forward. Letting an old answer rewind the schedule would undo real practice.

L

Leo: Say I answered a card on Monday offline, and again on Wednesday online. Monday's answer arrives on Thursday.

M

Maya: Wednesday's answer already set the next review date. Monday's answer is recorded for Monday's round, and your history shows it.

L

Leo: But the schedule stays where Wednesday put it.

M

Maya: Right. The history is complete, and the schedule only moves forward.

L

Leo: And if the round was abandoned? I left one half done last week.

M

Maya: An abandoned round still takes answers. Leaving a round is not a reason to lose what you practiced in it.

04What this design gives up

L

Leo: So what's the catch?

M

Maya: Offline, you can't get a new deck. And your streak doesn't update until the answers arrive.

L

Leo: Could I cheat with my clock?

M

Maya: A little. You can move your own answers inside the window. You can never touch anyone else's data.

L

Leo: Why clamp, and not just reject bad times?

M

Maya: Rejecting would lose practice the learner really did. Clamping keeps every answer, just with a believable time.

L

Leo: Is there a limit on how late an answer can be?

M

Maya: Late answers have no expiry. That keeps every practice, but an answer queued for a week can land a week later.

L

Leo: So when is the other design, rules on the device, the right choice?

M

Maya: When the user's own documents are the product: notes, design tools, shared docs. The "local-first" essay by Kleppmann and others argues for that there.

L

Leo: Because the data is theirs, not a result of our rules.

M

Maya: Right. For an app whose value is the server's rules, like ours, it would be heavy for little gain.

05Leo explains it back

M

Maya: A teammate on the mobile app asks if they need to port the scheduler to Kotlin. What do you say?

L

Leo: No. Our server owns the rules, so offline doesn't mean running them on the phone.

L

Leo: The client just records facts: this answer, this id, this time. It queues them in order, and a failed send stays at the head.

L

Leo: When they arrive, the server skips ids it already has. It clamps the time between the round's start and its own now.

M

Maya: And a late answer?

L

Leo: It counts on the round's day, and it never rewinds a schedule.

M

Maya: Tell them the price as well: offline, the learner sees nothing new until the answers sync. No new deck, no streak update. We accept that to keep one copy of the rules.

Summary in three lines

  1. With server-owned rules, offline means the client queues facts and the server decides when they arrive.
  2. Client-made ids make replay harmless, and a failed send stays at the head of the queue.
  3. Clamp reported times to the round's window, count late answers on the round's day, and never rewind a schedule.
