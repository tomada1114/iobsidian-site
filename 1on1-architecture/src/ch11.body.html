How do I defend a design decision in a review?

After this page you can write an architecture decision record that answers a reviewer before they ask. You can also keep it current as things change, and match a decision's effort to how hard it is to reverse.

About 8 min readAfter the design review

In this page

  • How does a record change over time?
  • How much effort should a decision get?

After the Tuesday review. Leo looks a little upset.

01The record, and its best part

L

Leo: That was rough. I proposed a cache in front of the card catalog. One of the reviewers asked, "What else did you consider, and what would make you change your mind?"

M

Maya: What did you say?

L

Leo: "It's faster." That's all I had. How do you always have an answer to that question?

M

Maya: Mostly because I wrote it down before the meeting. Do you know what an ADR is?

L

Leo: An architecture decision record. Honestly, I thought it was paperwork you write after the decision is made.

M

Maya: That's the usual view, and it's backwards. The record is how you think the decision through. The format comes from Michael Nygard.

L

Leo: What's in it?

M

Maya: A short file. A title, a status, the context, the decision itself, and the consequences, good and bad. The context is the situation behind the choice: its needs and limits.

L

Leo: That's less than I expected.

M

Maya: Our template adds a few parts: decision drivers, a "considered options" table with why each option lost, open questions, and dated sources. Drivers are the needs that push the choice one way.

L

Leo: Dated sources?

M

Maya: Prices and limits change. A number with a date tells a future reader how much to trust it.

L

Leo: Which part matters most in a review?

M

Maya: The "considered and rejected" table. It answers the reviewer's question before it's asked.

L

Leo: Do we have examples?

M

Maya: Plenty. Microservices were rejected because the operational cost gives us nothing at our size. A relational serverless database was rejected for its transaction limits.

L

Leo: So if someone asks "why not microservices?", the answer is already written, with the reason.

M

Maya: And the reason can be checked. That's what makes it persuasive, not the confidence in your voice.

02Status and change

L

Leo: What's the status part for?

M

Maya: Where the decision is in its life. Proposed means a recommendation, not yet confirmed.

L

Leo: And once it's confirmed?

M

Maya: Accepted: work builds on it. Later it may be Superseded, which means a later record replaced it and names it. Let me draw it.

M

Maya: Nygard's original advice is to never edit an accepted record. You only supersede it with a new one.

L

Leo: And we don't follow that?

M

Maya: We chose differently. An accepted record is rewritten in place to state the current decision. It gets a dated "amended" line that says what changed and why. We supersede only when the decision is replaced whole.

L

Leo: Isn't that rewriting history?

M

Maya: Git keeps the history. The file keeps the current truth, which is what a reader needs.

L

Leo: So I don't have to read three records to know what's true today.

M

Maya: Right. The cost is that to see how it changed, you read the diff. Some records are accepted only in part, and it says which part.

L

Leo: And a partial supersede?

M

Maya: One record's line about generating the API document was a decision of its own, replaced whole by a later record. The rest is still valid.

03Effort matches reversibility

L

Leo: Do we write a record for every choice, then?

M

Maya: No. Some decisions are cheap to undo: a date-formatting library, a dashboard layout. Spend little time on those.

L

Leo: And some are expensive.

M

Maya: The data shape, how learners are isolated from each other, authentication, infrastructure as code, fast deploys. Our rule is "build early what is hard to add later".

L

Leo: What came later, then?

M

Maya: A firewall, the email service, restore drills. Those arrived in a later phase that added guards for production, because adding them later is easy.

L

Leo: So the effort of a decision should match the cost of reversing it.

M

Maya: That's the rule. Jeff Bezos wrote about one-way and two-way doors: walk through two-way doors quickly, and slow down at one-way doors.

L

Leo: A one-way door being a choice you can't undo?

M

Maya: Right. Here it is on two axes.

L

Leo: A firewall isn't cheap to build, though.

M

Maya: True, it isn't free. But adding it later doesn't force us to rewrite anything. Changing how learners are isolated later would mean migrating every record.

04Presenting a decision

L

Leo: Okay. How do I actually say it in a review?

M

Maya: Say the drivers first, then the options and why each lost, then the consequences you accept. Then what would change your mind.

L

Leo: What does "what would change your mind" sound like?

M

Maya: Something concrete, like "We'll store this projection once a rule needs history we don't keep." A projection is data we keep ready for one screen. The sentence shows you know the limit of your decision.

L

Leo: Let me try my cache again.

M

Maya: Go.

L

Leo: Drivers: the home screen reads the catalog on every load. Options: a cache in memory, a cache service, or doing nothing.

M

Maya: And why each lost?

L

Leo: A cache service adds an extra network call and another service to run. Doing nothing... hmm. I never measured it.

M

Maya: That's the honest answer. The catalog is a snapshot, a fixed copy built with the app, so reading it may already be cheap.

L

Leo: So my first driver is a guess. I should measure first, and write "we'll add a cache if reads cross this number".

M

Maya: Exactly. Now your proposal has a reason, a rejected option and a trigger. Let me show you both versions side by side.

L

Leo: Can I ask about the rejected options? Doesn't listing them make the decision look weaker, like I wasn't sure?

M

Maya: It's the opposite. A reviewer trusts a choice more when they can see what it beat. A single option looks like the first idea you had.

L

Leo: But what if a reviewer likes one of the rejected ones better?

M

Maya: Then the table did its job. You argue about the reason it lost, which is concrete, instead of about taste.

L

Leo: And sometimes they'll be right.

M

Maya: Sometimes. Then you change the decision early, when it's cheap. That's a good outcome, not a defeat.

L

Leo: Should I write an ADR for it?

M

Maya: Records take time, and they can go stale. A team that writes one for every small choice has no time left to build. Write one when a reasonable reviewer could argue against the choice.

L

Leo: That reviewer clearly argued against it.

M

Maya: Then yes. A short one.

05Leo explains it back

M

Maya: Next time a reviewer asks what you rejected, what's your plan?

L

Leo: I'll have an ADR: the context, the options we rejected and why, and the consequences we accept.

L

Leo: We amend accepted records in place, with a date and a reason. We supersede only when the decision is replaced whole.

L

Leo: I'll spend decision time in proportion to how hard it is to reverse. Isolation and data shape early; a firewall can come later.

M

Maya: And in the room?

L

Leo: Drivers first, then the rejected options, then what would change my mind.

M

Maya: Careful with "it's faster": it was a guess that sounded like a reason but wasn't one. Measure before you name a driver.

Summary in three lines

  1. A decision record states the context, the options rejected and why, and the consequences accepted.
  2. Keep accepted records current with dated amendments, and supersede them only when the decision is replaced.
  3. Spend decision effort in proportion to the cost of reversing, and lead a review with drivers and rejected options.
