Should the timed drill wait for the grader?

After this page you can explain why a model call stays off a fast path, and how a background job grades instead. You can also explain why a stored result, not the model, makes a retry safe.

About 8 min readAt the whiteboard

In this page

  • Why keep the model off the drill?
  • How does a job key make grading safe to repeat?
  • Where does the job run, and what about duplicates?
  • What does grading later cost?

The whiteboard room. Leo's phone shows the drill's 30-second timer.

01The hot path

L

Leo: Here's my sketch. The learner sends a typed answer, the API calls Bedrock, and the verdict appears on the card.

M

Maya: And what worries you about it?

L

Leo: The call might take a few seconds. So I want a faster model, or streaming, to show the verdict sooner.

M

Maya: Before picking a model, look at the timer on your phone. How long does a learner get per card?

L

Leo: Their own setting, from 15 to 60 seconds. 30 by default.

M

Maya: And the drill never waits on anything slow today. What does your sketch add to that path?

L

Leo: Seconds of waiting. And a new way to fail, if Bedrock is slow or down.

M

Maya: This path, the one the learner feels on every card, is the hot path. A model call on it makes every card depend on a service we don't run.

L

Leo: What about streaming? The first words would appear sooner.

M

Maya: The verdict is what the learner needs, and it's one word. Streaming the explanation doesn't show it any sooner.

L

Leo: And a faster model only makes the wait shorter. It's still a wait.

M

Maya: Right. There's a second reason. Answers made offline arrive later, in batches.

L

Leo: So there's no card on screen to show a verdict on anyway.

M

Maya: So we record the answer and move on. Grades appear on the round summary and the recap, not on the card.

02The job and its key

L

Leo: So grading is a background job. How do we stop it grading the same answer twice?

M

Maya: The job has a key: grade: plus the answer id, plus the rubric version.

L

Leo: Answer ids are made by the client.

M

Maya: Right, and a resent batch of answers is already ignored. The job builds on that.

L

Leo: And the key does what?

M

Maya: The job's first successful result is stored under the key. A retry, a duplicate delivery or an offline replay finds it and reads it back.

L

Leo: Instead of calling the model again.

M

Maya: Yes. That's what idempotency means here: doing it twice has the same effect as doing it once.

L

Leo: I thought idempotency meant the model gives the same answer the second time.

M

Maya: That's the trap. The model's output isn't deterministic. Call it twice and you may get two different explanations, or even two verdicts.

L

Leo: So the safety comes from storage, not from the model.

M

Maya: Exactly.

L

Leo: What if two workers get the same job at the same moment? Both check, both find nothing.

M

Maya: The result is stored with a condition: only if nothing is under that key yet. One write wins, and the other worker reads the winner's result.

L

Leo: So one of them paid for a call that was thrown away.

M

Maya: Rarely, yes. We pay for the extra call, but the learner is charged once and sees one grade. Here's one job from start to finish.

L

Leo: Why is the rubric version in the key?

M

Maya: When the grading rules change, we may want to grade old answers again. A new rubric version means a new key.

L

Leo: So a regrade is a deliberate choice, not an accident.

M

Maya: Right. The old grades stay, under the old key.

L

Leo: What does the learner see before a grade is ready?

M

Maya: The round summary shows the answer as pending, and fills the grade in when it arrives. Usually that's before they finish reading.

03Where it runs

L

Leo: Where does the job actually run?

M

Maya: The same job function runs in two places. Locally, it runs inline, so you can test without a queue.

L

Leo: And in production?

M

Maya: On a worker Lambda, triggered by SQS. SQS is a message queue, and its standard queues deliver each message at least once.

L

Leo: At least once. So sometimes twice?

M

Maya: Sometimes twice. So duplicates are normal, not an error. The key handles them.

L

Leo: So the worker never needs to ask whether a message is new.

M

Maya: It only asks whether a result already exists for the key.

L

Leo: And if the model fails?

M

Maya: The job is retried. After a few failures, it lands in a dead-letter queue.

L

Leo: What's that?

M

Maya: A separate queue for messages that keep failing, where a person can look at them. The answer stays ungraded meanwhile, and the drill doesn't notice.

L

Leo: And once the cause is fixed?

M

Maya: We send those messages back to the main queue. The keys make that safe too.

L

Leo: What's "cost reserved" in the steps?

M

Maya: Before calling the model, the job reserves its cost in the learner's usage ledger, under the same key.

L

Leo: So a duplicate can't charge the learner twice either.

M

Maya: Right. The same key guards the result and the money.

04What grading later costs

L

Leo: What do we give up?

M

Maya: Immediacy. The learner waits until the round ends to see grades.

L

Leo: And more parts.

M

Maya: A queue, a worker, a result store, and "pending" states on screen. That's real work.

L

Leo: Is inline ever right?

M

Maya: When the user is waiting for that answer anyway. An "explain this" button, for example. The learner asked, and nothing else is timed.

L

Leo: Did we consider grading once per round, instead of per answer?

M

Maya: We weighed both. One call per round is fewer calls, but a longer one, and one failure affects every answer in it.

L

Leo: And per answer?

M

Maya: More calls, each small, and each can fail or retry alone. We measured both in a short experiment, a spike, before choosing, rather than arguing about it.

L

Leo: Have you seen inline go wrong?

M

Maya: At my last company, a support-ticket classifier ran inside ticket creation. Then the model provider slowed down.

L

Leo: And creating a ticket timed out.

M

Maya: For everyone. Nobody could file a ticket about the outage either.

L

Leo: What did they change?

M

Maya: They moved classification to a queue. Tickets kept flowing, and classification caught up when the provider recovered.

05Leo explains it back

M

Maya: Now explain the new design, instead of the sketch.

L

Leo: The drill never waits on a model. The answer is saved, and a job grades it later.

L

Leo: The job is keyed by answer id and rubric version. The first result is stored under that key.

L

Leo: So a retry or a duplicate just reads it back. We can't count on the model to repeat itself.

M

Maya: What changes when the rubric does?

L

Leo: A new key, so a regrade is on purpose.

M

Maya: The ledger belongs in that story as well: the same key reserves the cost, so a duplicate can't charge the learner twice.

Summary in three lines

  1. Keep model calls off a timed path; store the answer and grade it as a background job.
  2. Key the job by answer id and rubric version, and store the first result under that key.
  3. Retries and duplicates read the stored result, because the model won't repeat itself.
