How would we know a prompt change made grading worse?

After this page you can explain why an evaluation comes first, and how to build a gold set from what you have. You can also read agreement, flip rate, precision and recall.

About 8 min readAt the whiteboard

In this page

  • Why does the evaluation come first?
  • Where does a gold set come from?
  • Which numbers do we track?
  • What can go wrong with the numbers?

The whiteboard room. Leo holds a printout of ten graded answers.

01Why evals come first

L

Leo: I changed the grading prompt in my experiment. I read ten results, and they look better.

M

Maya: Better by how much? And on what?

L

Leo: Hmm. Better on these ten. By… they read more naturally?

M

Maya: That's an opinion. It may be right, but nobody can check it, including you next week.

L

Leo: I was going to add a proper evaluation once the feature works.

M

Maya: That's the usual order, and it's backwards. An LLM feature has no compiler, and often no single right output.

L

Leo: So without a number, every prompt tweak is an opinion.

M

Maya: And a regression ships silently. An evaluation is the test suite of a model feature. It's also our definition of "done".

L

Leo: Isn't that a lot of work up front? I thought you need thousands of labelled examples.

M

Maya: You need fewer than you think, and we already have most of them. Hamel Husain, and Chip Huyen in her book "AI Engineering", both put evaluation at the center.

L

Leo: Where do I start?

M

Maya: By reading real outputs, like you did, but with a pen. Write down each way a result fails.

L

Leo: Like "missed a wrong tense" or "called a typo incorrect"?

M

Maya: Exactly. Those names become the labels. Error analysis first, metrics after.

L

Leo: So my ten answers weren't wasted.

M

Maya: Not at all. They're the first page of the error list.

02Datasets from what exists

L

Leo: So where do the thousands of examples come from?

M

Maya: Look at what we already store. Every card has reviewed alternative answers, about two thousand in all.

L

Leo: Answers a person already accepted. So those are positives: the grader should call them correct or acceptable.

M

Maya: Right. Now take a reference answer and break it on purpose. Drop an article. Change the tense.

L

Leo: Then it's a negative, and we know which error tag it should get.

M

Maya: Yes, because we made the error. Those are perturbations.

L

Leo: Is a broken answer always wrong, though? Dropping "the" can still be fine in some sentences.

M

Maya: Not always. "She plays the piano" and "She plays piano" are both fine. So a person checks a sample of them, and the ones that turn out acceptable move to the positives.

L

Leo: Why not just use the alternatives? Two thousand is plenty.

M

Maya: They're all positives. A grader that accepts everything would score perfectly on them.

L

Leo: So without negatives, the set can't catch a grader that's too kind.

M

Maya: Right. Let me draw the whole set.

L

Leo: What counts as an attack?

M

Maya: Instructions hidden inside an answer. Off-task text. The wrong language. Empty answers, and very long ones.

L

Leo: And self-graded?

M

Maya: Typed answers that learners marked themselves. Noisier, but real.

L

Leo: What about the card reviewer? It's a different task.

M

Maya: It gets its own suite. We keep every deleted card with the reason it was rejected, hundreds of them. Those are labelled negatives.

L

Leo: So one suite per task.

M

Maya: And per language pair. Right now, only Japanese-to-English can get gold labels from someone on the team.

03The metrics

L

Leo: And then? What numbers do we compare?

M

Maya: A few. Here they are, with what each tells you.

MetricWhat it tells you
AgreementHow often the verdict matches the gold label
Flip rateHow often the verdict changes on the same input
Tag precisionOf the tags it gave, how many were right
Tag recallOf the real errors, how many it found
Cost, latencyWhat one graded answer costs, and how long it takes
L

Leo: Which one matters most for learners?

M

Maya: Agreement on the negatives. If the grader calls a wrong answer correct, the learner practises the mistake.

L

Leo: And why track cost and latency in an evaluation?

M

Maya: A prompt that grades slightly better but costs twice as much may not be worth it. The suite shows both, side by side.

L

Leo: Flip rate is new to me.

M

Maya: Grade the same answer several times. Count how often the verdict changes. The model isn't deterministic, so this is a real risk.

L

Leo: So a high flip rate points at the rubric, not only the model.

M

Maya: Often, yes. Now precision and recall, for the error tags. Picture four boxes.

L

Leo: Give me numbers.

M

Maya: An illustration. The grader gives 10 tags, and 8 are right. Precision is 0.8.

L

Leo: And recall?

M

Maya: The answers really have 12 errors, and it found 8 of them. Recall is about 0.67.

L

Leo: And F1?

M

Maya: One number that combines both, their harmonic mean. Here it's about 0.73. It drops fast if either one is low.

04What the numbers cost

L

Leo: Could a grader score well and still be bad?

M

Maya: Easily. A grader that calls everything "acceptable" agrees with every positive.

L

Leo: That's why the negatives and attacks are in the set.

M

Maya: Right. Any single metric can be cheated. Several together are much harder to fool.

L

Leo: What else?

M

Maya: Gold labels cost a person's time. And small sets are noisy, so one run can look better by luck.

L

Leo: So how do we compare?

M

Maya: Against a recorded baseline, not against one earlier run. The baseline is the current prompt's scores, stored with the suite.

L

Leo: Have you seen what happens without any of this?

M

Maya: At my last company, an HR policy question-and-answer bot launched on a good demo. Then the model was upgraded.

L

Leo: And it got worse?

M

Maya: It started citing old policies, confidently. Nobody noticed for weeks.

L

Leo: How did they find out?

M

Maya: An employee complained. Then the team built a gold set from real employee questions. The next regression was caught before release.

L

Leo: So for my prompt change, I run the suite and compare with the baseline.

M

Maya: Then you can say "better" with a number next to it.

05Leo explains it back

M

Maya: Say how grading gets approved from now on.

L

Leo: Grading ships against its test. Positives come from reviewed alternatives, negatives from perturbations, and attacks are test data too.

L

Leo: The card reviewer gets the rejected cards, each with its reason.

L

Leo: We track agreement, flip rate, tag precision and recall, cost and latency. So "better" is a number against a baseline.

M

Maya: And where does it all start?

L

Leo: With reading real outputs and naming how they fail.

M

Maya: You left out the cheat: a grader that calls everything "acceptable" scores perfectly on positives. So every metric needs a second one beside it.

Summary in three lines

  1. Build the evaluation before the feature; it is the test suite and the definition of done.
  2. Make the gold set from what you already store: accepted answers, broken ones, attacks and rejects.
  3. Track several metrics against a recorded baseline, because any one metric can be gamed.
