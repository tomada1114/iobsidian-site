One-on-Ones: AI in Production

Thirteen short conversations about putting LLM features into a real product. After them you can explain what a model call costs, how to shape and test it, and how to defend it. You can also say when an agent is worth it, and how it acts safely for a user.

13 pagesAbout 1 hour 45 minEnglish, B2 · dialogue

Who's talking

Maya is a staff engineer and the tech lead of Quickturn's platform. Leo was recently promoted to mid-level. He writes TypeScript every day with Claude Code. He wants to explain why the product's AI features are built the way they are. Each page is one of their one-on-ones.

Quickturn is a small company that makes a language-learning app, with a web app and iOS and Android apps on one API. Maya, Leo and Quickturn are fictional.

How the pages fit together

The pages

  1. Does every AI feature need to be an agent? — Pick the smallest shape that works, and save agents for open-ended tool use.
  2. What am I actually paying for in one LLM call? — Tokens in and out, and why the same prompt can answer differently.
  3. Why does Bedrock want an inference profile, not a model? — The profile decides where a call runs and which feature pays.
  4. Can an LLM return a type instead of a paragraph? — A schema shapes the output, and validation checks its meaning.
  5. Why not one complete(prompt) function for every feature? — Each task gets a port that owns its prompt, schema and evaluation.
  6. Should the timed drill wait for the grader? — Grade later as a job, and store the first result.
  7. How would we know a prompt change made grading worse? — A gold set and a few metrics turn opinions into numbers.
  8. Where do evals run: in CI, at night or in production? — Each place catches what the others miss.
  9. What if a learner types 'ignore your instructions'? — Limit what a successful attack can do, instead of betting on detection.
  10. Who pays for the tokens, and what stops a runaway bill? — A per-learner ledger counts money; a kill switch stops the account.
  11. What does AgentCore do that forty lines of code can't? — It rents you the production parts around the agent loop.
  12. When an agent acts for a learner, whose rights does it use? — From the token, never from a tool argument.
  13. Can a machine review the cards a machine wrote? — Only after it agrees with past human decisions, above all the rejections.

How to read this

Any page can be read on its own, in about 8 minutes. Maya is on the left, Leo on the right.
