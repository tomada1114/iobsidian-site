Can a machine review the cards a machine wrote?

After this page you can explain how a pipeline replaces content work done by hand. You can also explain how an automated reviewer earns trust, and why the rejections it catches matter most.

About 8 min readPairing at Leo's desk

In this page

  • How were cards made at first, and why change it?
  • What does the pipeline do?
  • How does a machine reviewer earn trust?
  • What does it cost, and where else does this apply?

Leo's desk. The card-review queue and the log of deleted cards are open.

01Where it started

L

Leo: Adding a hundred cards takes me an afternoon. Generate, read, fix, stamp. And more language pairs are coming.

M

Maya: What would you change?

L

Leo: Let the model review the cards too, and skip the person. If a model can write a card, another model can check it.

M

Maya: Maybe. First, let's look at how cards were made at the start.

L

Leo: On someone's laptop, with AI help.

M

Maya: Right. Then scripts checked them: a lint for format, and a near-duplicate check. A person reviewed each card and stamped it.

L

Leo: And only stamped cards reach learners.

M

Maya: Yes. And a deleted card keeps the reason it was rejected, in an append-only log.

L

Leo: What do the reasons look like?

M

Maya: "Unnatural phrasing." "A second correct answer isn't listed." "Tests the same point as another card." Short, but specific.

L

Leo: So what was wrong with that?

M

Maya: It worked, but it was manual, and ran one batch at a time. And for a language pair nobody on the team can read, there was no reviewer at all.

L

Leo: So the pipeline is about scale and about new pairs.

M

Maya: Both. Let me compare the two ways.

02The pipeline

L

Leo: Is the pipeline a cron job that calls the model in a loop?

M

Maya: That's the usual first version, and it breaks halfway with no record of where. We use Step Functions.

L

Leo: A state machine service?

M

Maya: Yes. You describe the steps, and AWS runs them, records each one, and retries a failed step on its own.

L

Leo: Which kind of workflow?

M

Maya: Standard. Each step runs exactly once, and a run can last up to a year. The other kind, Express, runs at least once and only up to five minutes.

L

Leo: A batch can take hours, so Standard fits.

M

Maya: Right. Here are the steps.

L

Leo: What does the plan step decide?

M

Maya: Which grammar points need cards, and how many, from the gaps in the catalog. The model doesn't choose what to teach.

L

Leo: How does it call the model?

M

Maya: Single calls use Step Functions' built-in Bedrock integration. Bulk generation uses batch inference, at half the on-demand price, with results back in S3.

L

Leo: And if one step fails at three in the morning?

M

Maya: That step retries on its own. A run is visible and can be resumed, so nobody reruns the whole batch.

L

Leo: Why a near-duplicate check at all? The model won't write the same card twice.

M

Maya: Not the same card, but often the same sentence with one word changed. A learner who sees both learns nothing new from the second.

L

Leo: Does the pipeline cost much?

M

Maya: Standard workflows are priced per state transition, with a free monthly allowance. As checked in September 2026, that's 2.5 cents per thousand, after 4,000 free.

L

Leo: So the model calls are still most of the cost.

M

Maya: By far. Which is why bulk goes to batch.

03Trusting the reviewer

L

Leo: Now the part I asked about. Can the automated review replace the person?

M

Maya: Only after it proves it agrees with the person. We already have the evidence.

L

Leo: The stamped cards?

M

Maya: About a thousand stamped cards are accepts. And hundreds of deleted cards, each with a written reason, are rejects.

L

Leo: So we run the reviewer on those and compare. If it agrees 95% of the time, we trust it?

M

Maya: Not so fast. Most of the set is accepts. A reviewer that accepts everything would agree most of the time too.

L

Leo: Because the rejects are the smaller group.

M

Maya: Right. So one cell of that comparison matters more than the others.

L

Leo: So catching the rejects matters most.

M

Maya: Yes. The deleted cards with reasons are the hardest test, because each one is a mistake a person already caught.

L

Leo: And the reasons help too?

M

Maya: They let us check more than accept or reject. If the reviewer rejects for a different reason than the person did, it may be right by luck.

L

Leo: And once it agrees?

M

Maya: It's trusted alone, but only for pairs no one on the team reads. A new pair reaches learners only after its review gate passes.

L

Leo: And Japanese-to-English?

M

Maya: A person can read it, so the person stays in the loop. The machine reviews, and a person supervises the stamp.

L

Leo: So my afternoon gets shorter, but it doesn't disappear.

M

Maya: For that pair, right. You'll read what the reviewer flagged, not every card from scratch.

04Costs, and the same pattern elsewhere

L

Leo: What's the catch?

M

Maya: Building and running a state machine. Batch results arrive hours later, not in seconds.

L

Leo: And the reviewer itself?

M

Maya: That's the subtle one. A reviewer from the same model family can share the writer's blind spots.

L

Leo: The same mistakes, approved by the same kind of mind.

M

Maya: So use a different prompt or a different model for review. Keep sampled human audits, and read what learners report.

L

Leo: Learners report cards?

M

Maya: Every card has a way to report a problem. Those reports feed the next audit, and sometimes a new reject reason.

L

Leo: Sampled audits even after it's trusted?

M

Maya: Always. Trust is measured, and a measurement can go stale when a model changes.

L

Leo: So a model upgrade means measuring the reviewer again.

M

Maya: Against the same accepts and rejects, before it reviews anything alone.

L

Leo: Does this pattern exist outside language cards?

M

Maya: Everywhere documents are processed: claims, invoices. An LLM extracts into a schema, and code validates it.

L

Leo: And the uncertain ones?

M

Maya: Low-confidence items go to a human queue. Teams raise the auto-approve threshold only after the machine matches past human decisions.

L

Leo: Same idea as our reviewer: earn trust from the record.

M

Maya: The record of past human decisions is the most valuable dataset those teams own.

05Leo explains it back

M

Maya: So, can the model review the cards? Give me the full answer.

L

Leo: Generation runs in a Step Functions pipeline, with batch inference for bulk work.

L

Leo: The automated reviewer earns trust by agreeing with the team's past stamps and rejections. Especially the rejections.

L

Leo: Only then may it review alone, and only for pairs nobody on the team can read.

M

Maya: And for Japanese-to-English?

L

Leo: A person still supervises the stamp.

M

Maya: Keep the blind-spot risk in the answer too: a different prompt or model for review, and human audits that never fully stop.

Summary in three lines

  1. Move content generation into a pipeline that records, retries and resumes each step.
  2. Let a machine reviewer work alone only after it agrees with past human decisions, above all the rejections.
  3. Guard against shared blind spots with a different reviewer prompt or model and sampled human audits.
