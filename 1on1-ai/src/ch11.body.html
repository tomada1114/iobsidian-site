What does AgentCore do that forty lines of code can't?

After this page you can explain what Amazon Bedrock AgentCore is, and what it isn't. You can also name its parts, say how each is billed, and say when an agent is worth running on it.

Coffee in the kitchen, standing. Leo spent the weekend on an agent tutorial.

01Laptop versus production

L

Leo: I built a demo tutor agent on my laptop this weekend. About forty lines, running in an hour.

M

Maya: What does it do?

L

Leo: It answers questions and looks up a fake learner's mistakes with one tool. So why does our real tutor run on a whole platform?

M

Maya: What do you think AgentCore is?

L

Leo: A framework that builds the agent for you, I guess. Or overkill: an agent is just a loop, so a Lambda function should do.

M

Maya: Neither. The loop really is small. What's large is everything around it.

L

Leo: But why not Lambda? It runs code, and we use it everywhere.

M

Maya: A tutor conversation can last a long time, with pauses. A Lambda invocation is stateless and stops after fifteen minutes at most.

L

Leo: So the conversation state would live somewhere else, and I'd rebuild it on every message.

M

Maya: And keep one learner's state away from another's. That's the first thing Runtime gives you.

L

Leo: Like what?

M

Maya: Keeping each learner's session separate, and alive through a long conversation. Checking who is calling.

L

Leo: Mine had one user: me.

M

Maya: Right. Then exposing tools safely, remembering across sessions, stopping forbidden calls, and tracing and scoring every session.

L

Leo: And AgentCore does all of that?

M

Maya: It offers those as parts. You can adopt them one at a time, with any framework or model. The agent code stays yours.

L

Leo: So our tutor is still our code.

M

Maya: TypeScript on Strands Agents, an open-source agent framework. AgentCore runs it and surrounds it. Let me draw the parts around it.

02The parts

L

Leo: What does each one actually replace?

M

Maya: Here's each part, its job, and what you'd build without it.

Runtime
A microVM per session, up to 8 hours. Without it: containers, routing, isolation.
Gateway
Many tool sources, one endpoint. Without it: tool plumbing per agent.
Identity
Who calls, and stored credentials. Without it: auth glue, secrets handling.
Memory
Short-term events, long-term records. Without it: a conversation store and summarizer.
Policy
Cedar rules checked before a tool call. Without it: checks scattered in tools.
Observability
OpenTelemetry traces and spans. Without it: tracing setup.
Evaluations
Online and on-demand scoring. Without it: a homemade judge pipeline.
L

Leo: A microVM per session? That's a small virtual machine?

M

Maya: Yes. Each learner's conversation runs in its own, so one session can't see another's memory or files.

L

Leo: And MCP is the tool protocol?

M

Maya: The Model Context Protocol, a standard way for agents to call tools. Gateway turns our application's commands and queries into MCP tools, through a Lambda target.

L

Leo: What does one request go through?

M

Maya: This path.

L

Leo: The identity check is our sign-in?

M

Maya: A JWT authorizer against our Cognito user pool. The same tokens the app uses.

L

Leo: And the Policy check? What does a rule look like?

M

Maya: Rules are written in Cedar, a policy language. For example: the tutor may read this learner's mistakes, but may not change their settings.

L

Leo: So even a tricked model can't call a tool the rule forbids.

M

Maya: Right. The Gateway refuses before the tool runs.

L

Leo: What do we keep in Memory?

M

Maya: The conversation only. The learner's progress stays in our own tables, where the rest of the app can read it.

03How it's billed

L

Leo: Is there a monthly fee?

M

Maya: No minimum fee. You pay per use, per part.

L

Leo: Per second of running?

M

Maya: Runtime is billed per second. And CPU isn't charged while the agent waits on I/O, like a model call. Memory is.

L

Leo: An agent spends most of its time waiting for the model.

M

Maya: So an idle agent costs little, and a chatty one scales with use. Gateway is priced per thousand tool calls, and Memory per thousand events, records and retrievals.

L

Leo: And Identity?

M

Maya: No extra charge when used through Runtime or Gateway. Here are the figures, with their date.

PartPrice, checked 2026-09-23
Runtime$0.1276 per vCPU-hour, $0.0169 per GB-hour
Gateway$0.005 per 1,000 tool calls
Memory$0.25 per 1,000 short-term events
L

Leo: So the model calls are still the big line.

M

Maya: Usually. AgentCore is the part around the model, and it's priced to be small next to it.

L

Leo: What happens to a session when the learner walks away?

M

Maya: By default it ends after fifteen minutes idle. A busy session can last up to eight hours.

04When it's worth it

L

Leo: So why not just host the agent on Lambda or ECS ourselves?

M

Maya: You can. Let's compare what you'd build.

L

Leo: What's the catch with AgentCore?

M

Maya: A new platform to learn, and AWS's shapes to live with. Runtime is arm64 only, and features change month to month.

L

Leo: Then when do we use it?

M

Maya: Our rule: a feature goes on AgentCore only when it needs multi-turn, open-ended tool use. That's why only the tutor runs there.

L

Leo: Grading doesn't qualify.

M

Maya: Grading is one call with a typed answer. A platform for sessions would add cost and nothing else.

L

Leo: How is the tutor deployed?

M

Maya: In its own agents stack in CDK. Runtime runs in public network mode, so still no VPC. The AgentCore CLI runs it locally and deploys it.

L

Leo: Wasn't there an older Bedrock Agents service?

M

Maya: There was. As of August 2026 it allows no new use, so new work goes to AgentCore.

L

Leo: Have you seen what the missing parts cost?

M

Maya: At my last company, the first internal agent was an operations assistant. It stayed a proof of concept for months.

L

Leo: Because the model wasn't good enough?

M

Maya: The model was fine. The missing parts were sign-in, session isolation and audit traces.

L

Leo: And once those were in place?

M

Maya: Production took weeks. The forty lines had been ready all along.

L

Leo: So my weekend demo is the easy part.

M

Maya: It's the part that proves the idea. Everything around it is what lets real learners use it safely, and that's the part to plan for.

L

Leo: Then I'll start by listing which of the seven parts our tutor actually uses.

M

Maya: All seven, today. A new agent might need only three or four at first.

05Leo explains it back

M

Maya: A teammate shows you their weekend demo and asks the same question. What do you say?

L

Leo: AgentCore doesn't write the agent. It rents you the production parts around the loop.

L

Leo: Isolated long sessions, a tool gateway, identity, memory, policy, tracing and evaluation. Each is billed by use.

L

Leo: It's worth it once you truly have an agent, and not before.

M

Maya: And our tutor?

L

Leo: Our own TypeScript on Strands Agents, running in Runtime, with our Cognito tokens at the door.

M

Maya: Keep the adoption order in mind too: you can take one part at a time. Nothing forces you to move everything at once.

Summary in three lines

  1. AgentCore doesn't build the agent; it provides the production parts around the loop.
  2. Its parts are billed by use, with no minimum fee, and can be adopted one at a time.
  3. Use it once a feature needs multi-turn, open-ended tool use, and not before.
